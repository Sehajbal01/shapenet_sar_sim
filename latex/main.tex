\Title{Open-Source SAR and Sonar Simulator in PyTorch Via Interposummation}

\Author{Alex Berian *, Sehajdeep Singh Bal, JhihYang Wu, Daniel Brignac and Abhijit Mahalanobis}

\abstract{There is currently a lack of open-source SAR and sonar simulators. \emph{RaySAR} supports only stripmap-mode imaging and forms images directly from geometry without simulating a received signal, while \emph{CVDomes} is limited to a few CAD models and spotlight mode. Both depend on outdated software such as MATLAB and POV-Ray, and neither simulates sonar. We present an \emph{open-source SAR and sonar simulator} implemented in \emph{PyTorch} with \emph{GPU acceleration} that renders four imaging modalities from a single ray-traced signal model: \emph{spotlight-mode SAR}, \emph{stripmap-mode SAR}, \emph{side-scan sonar}, and \emph{forward-looking sonar}. It supports arbitrary \texttt{.obj} 3D models, with configurable wavelength, sample rate, bandwidth, transmit waveform, and material properties. We simulate multi-bounce ray tracing to model realistic reflectivity, scattering, and absorption. We introduce an efficient low-pass filtering technique for summation and interpolation of ray returns, termed \emph{interposummation}, which accounts for the effective bandwidth and sample rate of the sensor. Each return keeps the round-trip phase of its path, so the simulated signals are complex-valued and support \emph{coherent imaging}. Interposummation accepts \emph{arbitrary transmit waveforms} through the effective window of their matched filter, so pulse-compression waveforms such as LFM chirps and Barker codes are simulated from the same ray trace as an ideal band-limited pulse. The simulator further supports radar cross section modeling, different noise types, and imperfect sensor trajectories. We test our simulator on ShapeNet car models with spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, and forward-looking sonar, and validate the stripmap-mode reconstruction against spotlight mode.}

\keyword{SAR; spotlight SAR; stripmap SAR; side-scan sonar; forward-looking sonar; pulse compression; ray tracing; simulator}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

SAR and sonar simulators are crucial for developing and validating radar and sonar algorithms when real-world data is unavailable or not feasible to process. By providing controlled, repeatable environments, simulators allow researchers to test imaging techniques, evaluate performance under different conditions, and benchmark novel methods without requiring extensive field experiments.

Most powerful simulators, such as XPatch \cite{xpatch4}, are either classified in some manner or proprietary to their respective developer. Open-source simulators like CVDomes \cite{cvdomes} and RaySAR \cite{raysar} lack flexibility, rely on outdated software, are each tied to a single SAR imaging mode, and do not simulate sonar. XPatch and the simulation behind CVDomes compute scattering with the shooting-and-bouncing-rays (SBR) method \cite{sbr}, which launches a dense grid of rays at the target and traces their reflections, and our ray tracer follows the same ray-launching approach.

To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first scalable PyTorch-based SAR and sonar simulation framework that allows for any 3D \texttt{.obj} model. Our simulator allows users to specify sample rate, bandwidth, transmit waveform, material properties (reflectivity, scatter, absorption), and sensor trajectory.
The primary functionality of the simulator is to use ray tracing combined with an interpolation/summation technique we call \emph{interposummation} to simulate signals that correspond to the sensor's wavelength, bandwidth, sample rate, and transmit waveform. We use two bounces from each ray. When a carrier wavelength is given, every ray return carries its round-trip phase, so the signals are complex-valued and SAR images are formed by coherent integration. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interposummation}, a low-pass summation and interpolation of ray returns that produces signals matched to the bandwidth and sample rate of the sensor and preserves the carrier phase of every return for coherent imaging. Unlike standard range compression, which filters a signal already sampled on a uniform grid, interposummation evaluates the band-limited response directly from irregularly spaced ray returns, so one step both interpolates each return onto the sample grid and sums all returns into each sample.
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary transmit waveforms.} Any transmit waveform enters the simulation through the effective window of its matched filter, so an ideal sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, a Barker-13 code, or any other sampled waveform is simulated from the same ray trace, with the range sidelobes of the corresponding pulse-compression system.
    \item \textbf{Four imaging modalities} from one simulator: spotlight-mode SAR \cite{spotlightmodesar}, stripmap-mode SAR, side-scan sonar (SSS), and forward-looking sonar (FLS). Every quantity in the simulator is expressed in spatial units, so radar and sonar share the same ray tracer, material model, and interposummation, and differ only in how rays are launched, how returns are weighted by the beam and the medium, and how the signals are formed into an image.
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:modality-comparison} highlights this flexibility, rendering a spotlight-mode SAR image by convolution backprojection (CBP), a stripmap-mode SAR image, an SSS image, and an FLS image of four different ShapeNet car models with the same simulator.

The overall process of our signal simulation is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim}. Our simulation
functions by simulating a grid of parallel rays with equal energy. The rays hit a triangle on the
\texttt{.obj} mesh. When a ray hits the mesh, some of the energy is reflected, some energy is
scattered back to the sensor, and some energy is lost to absorption. The reflected energy is part
of a second bounce ray that once again hits another triangle on the mesh with reflectivity,
scattering, and absorption. We gather scattered energy (Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.d) and its
corresponding traveled range (Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.c) into an energy-range scatter plot
(Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.e). From the energy-range scatter plot we use a sinc pulse, with width
corresponding to the radar system bandwidth, to sum and interpolate (interposummation) the
scatters into a single sample of the received signal (Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.f). The spacing
between each sinc pulse corresponds to the sample rate of the radar system. Unlike conventional
filtering of a sampled signal, this single step places every off-grid return at its exact range and
sums it with its neighbors, without first binning the returns onto a discrete range grid. Once there is a
received signal for each pulse at different azimuth and elevation angles, further analysis or
imaging (Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.b) can be done.

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}

To the best of our knowledge, there are only two other publicly available SAR data simulators.
RaySAR \cite{raysar} is a MATLAB repository for simulating stripmap-mode SAR images via ray
tracing. CVDomes \cite{cvdomes} is a MATLAB repository that simulates spotlight-mode SAR images
from a small set of CAD models. Each is limited to a single SAR imaging mode, neither simulates
sonar, and neither lets the user choose the transmit waveform: RaySAR forms images directly from
geometry without simulating a received signal, and CVDomes exports phase histories at a fixed band
and center frequency.

\subsection{CVDomes}
\label{sec:cvdomes}

CVDomes is a physics-calibrated pipeline where facet CADs are augmented with an explicit asphalt
ground plane and per-facet material labels (PEC, absorber, glass, plastic, asphalt). Monostatic
scattering is computed with a high-frequency SBR front end followed by a physical-optics
surface-current integral. Sampling is co-designed in both frequency and angle. In frequency, 512
bins span a bandwidth of $5.35$ GHz about a center frequency of $9.6$ GHz. In angle, azimuth is
computed in $0.5^\circ$ sectors, each extrapolated by $\pm 0.25^\circ$, to yield an effective step
of $0.0625^\circ$. The result is a dome-wide phase history covering $360^\circ$ of azimuth and
$30^\circ$--$60^\circ$ of elevation, exported as MATLAB ``data domes'' with fully polarimetric
channels (HH, HV, VV) for downstream imaging.



\subsection{RaySAR}
\label{sec:raysar}

RaySAR \cite{raysar} is a 3D SAR simulation tool that generates SAR-like images from detailed 3D
object models. The framework is built on a modified version of the POV-Ray tracer, using it to
simulate interactions between electromagnetic waves and surfaces of objects. Instead of
transmitting pulses or collecting real echoes, RaySAR performs geometric ray tracing to determine
which surfaces are illuminated, and estimates the backscatter of each surface from its local
incidence geometry relative to the radar sensor. This simulated backscatter is then projected into
the SAR slant-range coordinate system, producing radar-like images that capture geometric effects
such as layover, shadowing, and foreshortening. Multiple reflections are traced and separated into
single-, double-, and higher-bounce image layers. Users can define synthetic flight paths and
viewing geometries \cite{drozdowicz2021open} to analyze how different radar positions influence
the resulting image. RaySAR is therefore a forward-modeling tool: it predicts how 3D scenes would
appear in SAR imagery rather than simulating raw signal acquisition.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
A \texttt{.obj} file is a collection of $V$ vertices in 3D space and $F$ triangle faces. Our goal
is to produce a SAR or sonar image of the mesh from $P$ pulses (pings, in sonar) of a sensor with a
given transmit waveform, bandwidth, and sample rate. A signal needs to be simulated for each pulse
from a known azimuth angle $a$ and elevation angle $e$. From the simulated signals, we form
spotlight-mode SAR, stripmap-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, or forward-looking sonar images, or do
other analyses.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Signal Simulation}


The general process for simulating signals is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim}. We cast a grid of
$R_h \times R_w$ parallel rays that originate on a grid of size $W_g \times H_g$. The center of the
grid intersects a line from the origin for a given azimuth $a$ and elevation $e$ angle. 
Azimuth $a$ is defined as the angle from the $x$ to the $y$ axis, and elevation $e$ is the 
angle from the $x$-$y$ plane to the $z$ axis. The grid is orthogonal to this line defined by the 
azimuth/elevation. This set of rays can be viewed as a simulated wavefront. The size of the ray 
grid only needs to be big enough to cover the ground space desired for imaging.

When a ray makes contact with a surface, a fraction of its energy is reflected into the next path
of the ray, a fraction is absorbed, and a fraction is scattered. We model these three fractions as
$R,A,S$. Scattering is the only component that returns energy to the receiver. The fractions can be
assigned to each of the $F$ faces of the mesh by a \texttt{.mp} file that accompanies the
\texttt{.obj}, with one line of $(R,A,I,D,S)$ per triangle (Section \ref{sec:per-triangle-materials}).
Without one, we use only two materials, one for the object and one for the ground, whose values are
listed in Table \ref{tab:materials}.

The three fractions of a surface add up to 1, so that a surface never returns more energy than it
receives:
\begin{equation}
    R+S+A = 1
    .
\end{equation}
Only scattering is considered for returned energy. Scattering occurs in two ways, directional
intensity $I$ and diffuse scattering $D$, which split the scattered fraction:
\begin{equation}
    D + I = 1
    .
\end{equation}
Material properties that do not satisfy these constraints are divided by their sums before ray
tracing. Table \ref{tab:materials} gives the default materials in both forms. The car scatters about a
third of the incident energy, almost entirely in the directional lobe, so it returns strong glints
near the mirror direction and little elsewhere. The ground scatters weakly and mostly diffusely, so it
returns a dim, nearly uniform clutter that does not mask the car.

Returned energy from a particular ray $E_r$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Big(m_I\cos({\theta/2})^\alpha + m_D\Big)
\end{equation}
where $E_{in}$ is the ray energy going into the surface, $\theta$ is the angle between the 
direction of the reflected ray $\vec{u}_r$ and the direction to the line of sight $\vec{u}_l$,
$\alpha$ sets the width of the directional lobe, and $m_I,m_D$ are multipliers that conserve the
scattered energy.

We solve for the multipliers by ensuring that energy scattered in all directions integrates to
$SE_{in}$:
\begin{equation}
	S E_{in} = \oiint\limits_{H} S E_{in} \Big(m_I\cos({\theta/2})^\alpha + m_D\Big)d\Omega
\end{equation}
where $H$ denotes the hemisphere of directions from the surface.

\begin{equation}
    1 = m_I \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega + m_D \oiint\limits_{H} 1 d \Omega
\end{equation}

Now we can accurately ensure that the total amount of energy diffuse scattered is $D$ and the 
total amount of energy directionally scattered is $I$.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:directional-int}
    I = m_I \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega
    .
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    D =  m_D \oiint\limits_{H} 1 d \Omega = 2 \pi m_D
    .
\end{equation}

Using the half angle trigonometry identity,
\begin{equation}
    \cos\frac{\theta}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\cos \theta}{2}}
    ,
\end{equation}
and we can express $\cos\theta$ using dot product
\begin{equation}
    \cos\theta = \vec{u}_{l}\cdot \vec{u}_{r}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{u}_l$ is the line-of-sight vector which is the directional vector from the point of 
contact of the ray and where the radar is. $\vec{u}_r$ and other directional vectors are expanded 
as 
\begin{equation}
    \vec{u}_r = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \cos(a_r)\cos(e_r) \\
            \sin(a_r)\cos(e_r) \\
            \sin(e_r)
        \end{bmatrix}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $a_r$ and $e_r$ are the azimuth/elevation angles relative to the surface for the directional
vector $\vec{u}_r$. The integral in (\ref{eq:directional-int}) can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:big-int}
    \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega = \int_{e_l=0}^{e_l=\pi/2}\int_{a_l=0}^{a_l=2 \pi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{u}_l}{2}}^\alpha \cos(e_l) da_lde_l
    .
\end{equation}
The integral in (\ref{eq:big-int}) is identical for any relative azimuth angle of the reflected
ray $a_r$, so it depends only on the relative elevation angle of the reflected ray $e_r$. It is
impractical to compute this integral for each ray hitting each surface on the object mesh, so we
seek a cheaper equivalent. Computing $e_r$ directly requires trigonometry, but a quantity that is
one-to-one with $e_r$ follows from a single dot product,
\begin{equation}
    \cos\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}-e_r\bigg) = \vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{n}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{n}$ is the normal vector of the surface. While $\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n}$ is cheap to
evaluate, the integral itself remains expensive, so we approximate it with a polynomial
$f_{\alpha,poly}$ that is specific to $\alpha$,

\begin{equation}
    f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n}) \approx \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega
    .
\end{equation}

With the integrals computed, the multipliers are given by
\begin{equation}
    m_D = \frac{D}{2 \pi}
    ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    m_I = \frac{I}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})}
    ,
\end{equation}
which yields our final expression for returned energy as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:almost}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Bigg(\frac{I \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})} + \frac{D}{2\pi}\Bigg)
\end{equation}
The $\theta / 2$ in the numerator of (\ref{eq:almost}) refers to the angle between the direction
back to the radar and the direction of the reflected ray, whereas the $\theta / 2$ in
(\ref{eq:big-int}) refers to the angle between the reflected ray direction and all possible
line-of-sight vectors.
Again, to avoid trigonometric computations for each ray, we substitute $\cos(\theta/2)$ to give
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:returned-energy}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Bigg(\frac{I \sqrt{\frac{1+\vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{u}_l}{2}}^\alpha}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})} + \frac{D}{2\pi}\Bigg)
\end{equation}

The energy reflected into the next path of the ray after bouncing is given by
\begin{equation}
    E_{out} = RE_{in}
    ,
    \label{eq:reflected-energy}
\end{equation}
where $E_{out}$ is the energy carried into the next path of the ray after the bounce, and $R$ is
the reflectivity of the triangle in the object mesh that has been hit. The energy of the first
path of each ray is set to unity, and (\ref{eq:reflected-energy}) supplies $E_{in}$ for every path
thereafter. Every modality divides its returns by the number of transmitted rays $R_hR_w$, so the
sensor transmits a total energy of 1 (Section \ref{sec:units}). This is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:terminology}.
If the next path of a ray hits a location that is out of the line-of-sight, it is not considered
for returned energy. If a ray has no intersections, it is also not considered. We only consider
two-bounce returns, so each ray contributes at most two returns.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 80 246 0, clip, width=0.6\textwidth]{figs/terminology.pdf}
  \caption{Visual example of terminology for (\ref{eq:returned-energy}).}
  \label{fig:terminology}
\end{figure}

Ray tracing the full grid therefore yields a set of $N$ energy-range pairs $(E_k,z_k)$,
where $E_k$ is the \emph{return weight} of the $k^{th}$ return, its returned energy
(\ref{eq:returned-energy}) divided by $R_hR_w$, and
$z_k$ is the total distance traveled by that ray from and back to the ray grid plane. This
ray-launching stage follows standard SBR practice \cite{sbr}. Our contribution lies in how the
resulting returns are turned into a sampled signal, described next. We treat the
returned energy from ray tracing as a continuous spatial signal made up of Dirac
delta functions.
\begin{equation}
    g(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\delta(z-z_k)
    .
    \label{eq:returned-scatters}
\end{equation}
This set of energy-range pairs is called the energy-range scatter plot. The Fourier transform is
then given by
\begin{equation}
    G(Z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{- j 2 \pi z_k Z}
\end{equation}
which has infinite bandwidth, meaning it is impossible to sample without aliasing. We want to
interpolate and sum $g(z)$ with sufficiently low bandwidth and sample rate that we do not see
the jagged effects of the Dirac delta functions.

A radar system typically uses pulse compression techniques to improve range resolution. In short,
the bandwidth of the received signal is approximately the bandwidth of the radar pulse used. The
temporal bandwidth and sampling rate of the received signal are $B_t$ and $F_t$ respectively. They
are related to the \emph{spatial bandwidth} and \emph{spatial sampling rate} as $B_s = 2B_t/c$ and
$F_s = 2F_t/c$, where $c$ is the propagation speed, $3\times10^8$~m/s for radar and about
$1500$~m/s for sound in water. The corresponding spatial sample
spacing is $T_s = 1/F_s$.

We propose to simultaneously interpolate and sum the received set of scatters in
(\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) using a sinc pulse train given by
\begin{equation}
    p(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinc\big(B_s(z-nT_s)\big)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\sinc(x) = \sin(\pi x)/\pi x$. The simulated digital signal evaluated at the range $z_o$ is
then given by
\begin{equation}
    s(z_o) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sinc\big(B_s(z-z_o)\big)\sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\delta \bigg(z-\frac{z_k}{2} \bigg) dz
    .
\end{equation}
We divide the scatter's range $z_k$ by $2$ because of the two-way travel, which aligns the
samples with their position in space. Then with the sifting property $s(z_o)$ is reduced to
\begin{equation}
    s(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\sinc\Bigg(B_s\bigg(z_o-\frac{z_k}{2}\bigg)\Bigg)
    .
    \label{eq:interposummation}
\end{equation}
The $n^{th}$ sample of the resulting digital signal corresponds to the range $z=nT_s$. The sinc
function acts as both a low-pass interpolation filter and a summation over nearby samples in the
energy-range scatter. This differs from standard interpolation, which resamples a signal already
given on a uniform grid, and from standard range compression, which filters such a sampled signal.
Here the inputs are $N$ returns at arbitrary continuous ranges $z_k/2$, often many per sample
spacing, and (\ref{eq:interposummation}) interpolates each of them onto the sample grid and sums
them in a single step. We name the processing in (\ref{eq:interposummation}) \emph{interposummation}.
Since interposummation is linear in $E_k$, it applies unchanged to complex returns that carry their
carrier phase (\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}), so the simulated signals preserve phase for coherent
imaging.


\subsection{Units and Scale}
\label{sec:units}

Every length in the simulator, including the mesh, the ray grid, the sensor distance, the
wavelength $\lambda$ and the range axis, is expressed in one arbitrary unit $\ell$, and $B_s$ and
$F_s$ are in cycles per $\ell$. The ShapeNet meshes are normalized so that a car is about
$0.9\ell$ long. Time never enters the simulation, so the propagation speed $c$ appears only when
converting $B_s$ and $F_s$ to temporal frequencies. The simulated signals depend only on ratios of
lengths, such as the range resolution $1/B_s$ to the size of the car or $\lambda$ to the ray
spacing, so a scene in $\ell$ maps to any physical scale. Table \ref{tab:units} converts the
defaults for a car about $4.5$~m long, or $1\ell \approx 5$~m.

The bandwidth corresponds to a high-resolution radar, and to a low-frequency sonar. The SAR
wavelength does not: an X-band radar at this scale has $\lambda \approx 3$~cm $\approx 0.006\ell$.
A short wavelength needs the ray spacing to sample its phase, $\Delta < \lambda/(4\sin\phi)$
(Section \ref{sec:validation}), or the returns of neighboring rays interfere at random within each
pulse. At the default $208 \times 208$ rays ($\Delta \approx 0.006\ell$) the images break down at
$\lambda = 0.02\ell$ and below (Figure \ref{fig:wavelength} of Appendix \ref{app:sar-examples}), and X-band would need about four
times the ray density along each axis, or $15$ times the rays. Coherent integration across pulses
would also need about $15$ times the $64$ default pulses to sample the aperture without aliasing.
We therefore use a wavelength much longer than a physical radar's for the same scene, which keeps
the phase well sampled at an affordable ray count.

Energy is also unitless. The sensor transmits a total energy of 1, split equally among its rays,
so a return weight $E_k$ is the fraction of the transmitted energy that the $k^{th}$ return brings
back to the sensor, and a signal or image value is a sum of such fractions. The coherent sum
(\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}) adds the return weights as field amplitudes, and $|s|^2$ is taken as
received power, as in the flat-plate validation of Section \ref{sec:validation}. The physically
consistent amplitude would be $\sqrt{E_k}$. Using $E_k$ instead preserves where returns fall and
their phases, but squares their relative amplitudes, which doubles the dynamic range of the images
in dB. No antenna gain, transmit power or
receiver noise figure is modeled, so signal values are relative to the transmitted energy rather
than calibrated in watts.

\subsection{Arbitrary Transmit Waveforms}
\label{sec:waveforms}

Interposummation as written in (\ref{eq:interposummation}) simulates an ideal band-limited pulse.
Real radar and sonar systems instead transmit pulse-compression waveforms and match-filter the
echoes, and the range sidelobes of the waveform shape the final image. We therefore generalize
interposummation to an arbitrary transmit waveform. We can express a received continuous signal $r(z)$ from our scatters model in
(\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) with an arbitrary transmitted waveform $x(z)$ as
\begin{equation}
    r(z) = x(z) * g(z)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $*$ is convolution. It is common in practical radar systems for the transmitted signal
$x(z)$ to be a pulse compression waveform such as an LFM chirp or Barker-13. To compute the
recovered signal we use the matched filter, which is the time reverse and complex conjugate of
$x(z)$,
\begin{equation}
    h(z) = x^*(-z)
    .
\end{equation}
The received and recovered match-filtered digital signal $s_{mf}(z_o)$ evaluated at the range $z_o
$ is then given by 
\begin{equation}
    s_{mf}(z_o) = h(z)*r(z) |_{z=z_o}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    = h(z)*x(z)*g(z) |_{z=z_o}
     \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    =w(z)*g(z) |_{z=z_o}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    =\sum_{k=1}^{N}E_kw\bigg(z_o-\frac{z_k}{2}\bigg)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $w(z) = h(z) * x(z)$ is the effective windowing function, and $|_{z=z_o}$ means to evaluate the expression at $z=z_o$. Although $w(z)*g(z)$ has the form of conventional matched filtering, it is never computed by convolving a sampled received signal. Since $g(z)$ is a sum of off-grid delta functions, the matched-filter output is evaluated in closed form at each sample $z_o$, so the same sum performs both the interpolation and the summation of the returns. One may view the ideal band-limited digital signal in (\ref{eq:interposummation}) as the case $w(z)=\sinc(B_s z)$, which has no frequency domain side-lobes.

Each transmit waveform therefore replaces the ideal sinc of interposummation with a different effective window $w(z)$. Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows} compares the effective windows produced by a sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, and a Barker-13 code, all designed for the same spatial bandwidth $B_s$. In the range domain (Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}, left) the sinc yields the narrowest mainlobe but slowly decaying range sidelobes, while the Gaussian pulse trades a wider mainlobe for the complete suppression of sidelobes. The LFM chirp and Barker-13 waveforms, which are widely used in practical radar systems, achieve a mainlobe comparable to the sinc while exhibiting the characteristic range sidelobes of pulse compression.

In the frequency domain (Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}, right) the sinc window fills the band $\pm B_s/2$ with a sharp cutoff, whereas the Gaussian rolls off smoothly and the LFM and Barker-13 spectra fill the band with some ripple. Because interposummation is agnostic to the choice of $w(z)$, our simulator can substitute any of these effective windows to emulate the range response of a real radar's or sonar's pulse-compression waveform. The waveform is not limited to these four: any transmit waveform $x(z)$ sampled on a fine range grid is supported, since its effective window is computed once as the autocorrelation of $x(z)$ and then linearly interpolated at the range offset of every return. The waveform therefore adds no ray tracing, and every return costs the same as with the ideal sinc. The effective window is shared by all four imaging modalities.

\subsection{Imaging Modalities}
\label{sec:modalities-overview}

The simulated signals are independent of how they are imaged, and the simulator forms images in
four modalities, summarized in Table \ref{tab:modalities} and detailed in Appendix
\ref{sec:modalities}. All four share the ray tracer, the material model of
(\ref{eq:returned-energy}), and interposummation with any effective window $w(z)$, so the range
resolution of every modality is set by the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of the transmit waveform. They
differ in how the rays are launched, how the returns are weighted, and how the signals are formed
into an image.

\textbf{Spotlight-mode SAR} casts the parallel ray grid of Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim} from each
position of a circular or linear trajectory, aimed at the scene origin, and forms the image by
convolution backprojection \cite{spotlightmodesar}. \textbf{Stripmap-mode SAR} images the signals
of a linear trajectory by filtered backprojection along the exact range from each sensor position
to each pixel, with the ramp filter applied after demodulation so that it nulls the carrier rather
than baseband. When a wavelength is given, both demodulate each pulse and integrate the
complex-valued pulses coherently, so the image is formed from the phase of the returns as well as
their magnitude.

The sonars replace the planar wavefront with a point source. Each ping casts a fan of rays from the
sensor, every return is attenuated by spherical spreading, and the beam pattern of the sensor
weights each return on both transmit and receive. \textbf{Side-scan sonar} transmits its pings
along a straight track with a fixed beam, adds absorption in the water and a time-varying gain, and
stacks the range profiles along the track. \textbf{Forward-looking sonar} transmits from a single
position and steers its beam across the scene. Since the sensor does not move, the beam is applied
after ray tracing, and all $P$ pings share a single ray trace.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We first compare a constant material with per-triangle materials on a CVDomes car, then validate the SAR forward model on point scatterers, a sphere, and a flat plate. The following experiments use spotlight-mode SAR as described in the Jakowatz book \cite{spotlightmodesar}, vary the bandwidth and sample rate to show where the signal model breaks down, and then measure the compute time on a GPU and a CPU. The remaining experiments vary the beam width and bandwidth of the forward-looking and side-scan sonars. Unless specified otherwise, the SAR experiments use the default parameters shown in Table \ref{tab:params} of Appendix \ref{app:params}, and are on the car model from the ShapeNet \cite{shapenet} cars dataset shown in Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.a.

\subsection{Validation Against Closed-Form References}
\label{sec:validation}

We first image $25$ ideal point scatterers on a $5 \times 5$ grid spanning $\pm 0.4$ in
spotlight-mode SAR at the default parameters of Table \ref{tab:params}. Each scatterer is injected
directly into interposummation (\ref{eq:interposummation}) at its true range with the phase
$e^{j4\pi R/\lambda}$, bypassing the ray tracer (Figure \ref{fig:validation}b). Every target is
imaged as a compact peak within $0.0025$ of its true position, $0.27$ of a pixel of the default
$128 \times 128$ image, and the phase of each return matches $e^{j4\pi R/\lambda}$ across the
aperture to $1.7\times10^{-6}$ degrees RMS, so the simulated signals preserve phase and the pulses
integrate coherently. A single scatterer at the scene origin
(Figure \ref{fig:validation}a) is imaged as a peak $0.013$ wide at $-3$~dB in both range and
cross-range, with sidelobes that form an X along the wavefronts of the first and last pulses, where
the $135^\circ$ aperture ends. Along range, each return takes the shape of the
effective window $w(z)$ of Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}, so the range resolution and
sidelobes are those of the transmit waveform. In the image, the magnitude of the signal aliases at
the default $F_s = 2B_s$, which narrows the impulse response by up to $42\%$ relative to an analytic
backprojection; at $F_s \ge 8B_s$ the two agree within $4\%$.

In Figure \ref{fig:sphere-size}, we ray-trace a sphere at five successively halved scales ($1$ to
$1/16$ of the mesh's native size), with the ground plane removed and the pulse count raised to

Where the point targets bypass the ray tracer, the flat plate tests the phase of ray-traced returns.
We ray-trace a square plate of side $L = 0.4$ and area $A = L^2$, facing a sensor at
elevation $0^\circ$ and range $1.3$ with $512 \times 512$ rays, and sweep the sensor azimuth by
$\phi$ over $\pm 12^\circ$. We compare the squared magnitude of the coherent sum of its returns,
$\big|\sum_k E_k e^{j2\pi z_k/\lambda}\big|^2$, with the physical-optics radar cross section
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(\phi) = \frac{4\pi A^2}{\lambda^2}\cos^2\phi \,
    \sinc^2\!\bigg(\frac{2L\sin\phi}{\lambda}\bigg)
    .
    \label{eq:plate-rcs}
\end{equation}
The simulated glint pattern overlays (\ref{eq:plate-rcs}) at both wavelengths
(Figure \ref{fig:validation}c), with $-3$~dB widths of $3.170^\circ$ and $1.588^\circ$ against
$3.171^\circ$ and $1.586^\circ$ and peak sidelobes of $-13.42$ and $-13.30$~dB against $-13.30$ and
$-13.27$~dB, and at normal incidence the simulated $\sigma$ grows as $A^{1.98}$ against $A^2$. The
match holds as long as the ray spacing $\Delta$ samples the phase across the plate,
$\Delta < \lambda/(4\sin\phi)$. Because all rays return in phase at normal incidence, the simulated
$\sigma$ does not depend on $\lambda$, so it matches (\ref{eq:plate-rcs}) only up to the aperture
gain $4\pi/\lambda^2$.

\subsection{Bandwidth and Ray Density}

In Figure \ref{fig:fsbw}, we analyze the effect of varying the spatial sample rate and bandwidth. Each plot indicates the sample rate in its caption, and we set the spatial bandwidth $B_s = F_s$ equal to the sampling frequency. The images are shown in dB scale with a shared DC maximum. As expected, the image resolution improves as both bandwidth and sample rate increase. However, an interesting artifact appears at $F_s = 512$. This occurs because, at high sampling rates, the discrete samples begin to capture the fine-scale variations of the continuous returned-signal model described in (\ref{eq:returned-scatters}). Since this signal model is composed of a series of delta functions, oversampling reveals its discontinuous nature rather than producing a smooth approximation, and the reconstructed SAR image degrades.

  \label{fig:fsbw}
\end{figure}

We further look into this problem in Figure \ref{fig:signals-square}. The figure presents the energy-range scatter plot (Figure \ref{fig:signals-square}.a) together with the corresponding interposummed signals at different bandwidth/sample rates (Figure \ref{fig:signals-square}.b-d). The signal appears clean at $F_s = B_s = 128$, whereas increasing the sampling rate and bandwidth to $256$ and $512$ results in the emergence of distinct artifacts from the Dirac delta functions as described in (\ref{eq:returned-scatters}).

There are two ways to mitigate this effect: increasing the number of rays $(R_h, R_w)$, and decreasing the spatial bandwidth $B_s$. A denser ray grid allows for more scatters near the center of the sinc pulses in interposummation, which reduces the variable sums between neighboring sincs. Decreasing the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of the interposummation accomplishes the same goal, but by widening the sinc pulses to accumulate more scatters instead of adding more scatters. The trade-offs between the two solutions are that increasing the ray density increases the computational complexity, and decreasing the bandwidth reduces image quality.

\subsection{Forward-Looking Sonar Beam Width}

The remaining experiments test the two sonar modalities of Section \ref{sec:modalities-overview}
(Appendices \ref{sec:forward-looking} and \ref{sec:side-scan}) on a second car from the ShapeNet
cars dataset. They use the same ray tracer, material model, and interposummation as the SAR
experiments above. The car scatters almost entirely into the directional lobe and the
seafloor mostly diffusely, so the surfaces of the car that face the sensor return brightly, while
the rest of the car and its acoustic shadow are dark against the seafloor.

In Figure \ref{fig:fls-beam-width}, we vary the azimuth beam width $\beta$ of the forward-looking
sonar of Appendix \ref{sec:forward-looking} logarithmically from $0.1^\circ$ to $10^\circ$, with the
other parameters at their defaults (Table \ref{tab:fls-params}). The sensor sits at a range of
$\lVert\vec{v}\rVert = 7.5$ and an elevation of $e = 40.6^\circ$, and steers $P = 128$ pings across
$\Theta_p = 9.15^\circ$, one every $0.072^\circ$. Its fan of $R_h \times R_w = 550 \times 350$ rays
spans $\Theta_h = 23^\circ$ in elevation and $\Theta_w = 10^\circ$ in azimuth. The $M = 128$ rows
span $S_\rho = 1.2$, from $\rho_{near} = 6.9$ to $\rho_{far} = 8.1$, the pulse is Gaussian with
$B_s = 32$, and no wavelength is given. Each panel is shown in dB relative to its own peak, with a
floor of $-60$~dB.

At $0.1^\circ$, the car and its shadow form a sharp silhouette on the seafloor: the near side of the
car is bright, and its roof and the shadow behind it are dark. Even the narrowest beam spans
$1.4$ ping spacings, so the beam rather than the pings sets the azimuth resolution at every width,
and the $10$--$90\%$ width of the edge of the shadow grows with it, from $0.15^\circ$, two pings, at
$0.1^\circ$ to $0.36^\circ$ at $0.32^\circ$ and $1.06^\circ$ at $1^\circ$. At $3.16^\circ$ the beam
is wider than the $3^\circ$ shadow, whose darkest point rises to $42\%$ of the amplitude of the
seafloor, and at $10^\circ$ the shadow is gone and the returns of the car spread into streaks of
constant range across the image. Range is unaffected, since it is resolved by $B_s$. The peak, on
the near side of the car, grows roughly in proportion to $\beta$ up to $1^\circ$, from
$6.8\times10^{-9}$ to $5.6\times10^{-8}$. The fan is the same at every width, and the beam pattern
peaks at $1$ rather than integrating to $1$, so a wider beam sums the returns of more rays into
each ping. Beyond $1^\circ$ the growth slows, to $1.0\times10^{-7}$ at $10^\circ$, as the beam comes
to cover most of the $10^\circ$ fan.

\subsection{Forward-Looking Sonar Bandwidth}

In Figure \ref{fig:fls-fsbw}, we vary the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of the forward-looking sonar from
$4$ to $512$ in powers of two, with $F_s = 2B_s$ and the other settings of Figure
\ref{fig:fls-beam-width} at their defaults, so $\beta = 0.1^\circ$. The Gaussian pulse has an
amplitude full width at half maximum of $0.62/B_s$, which spans $16$ rows at $B_s = 4$ and one row at
$B_s \approx 66$.

At $B_s = 4$, the car is smeared in range into a bright blob, and the edges of its shadow are soft.
The car sharpens in range with the bandwidth, and by $B_s = 32$ its outline and glints are resolved.
The seafloor is a continuum of returns, of which a narrower pulse gathers fewer, so its amplitude
halves with every doubling of $B_s$. The peak, on the near side of the car, is more compact in
range and falls more slowly, by a factor of $0.55$--$0.72$ per doubling, from $2.1\times10^{-8}$ at
$B_s = 4$ to $7.3\times10^{-10}$ at $512$. The seafloor therefore darkens relative to the peak, from
$-27$~dB at $B_s = 4$ to $-36$~dB at $64$.

From $B_s = 128$ on, concentric rings centered on boresight appear in the seafloor. This is the ray
density effect of Figure \ref{fig:signals-square}. The pulse has become narrower than the range
spacing between the seafloor returns of neighboring rays, which grows with range as the fan meets
the seafloor at ever shallower angles, from $0.0050$ at the near edge of the image to $0.0078$ at
the far edge, against a pulse width of $0.0048$ at $B_s = 128$ and $0.0024$ at $256$. Each signal
then resolves the returns of individual rays, and the dark rings trace the pixels whose ranges fall
between them. Beside the car, the standard deviation of the seafloor is $0.8$~dB for
$B_s \le 64$, but $1.6$~dB at $B_s = 128$, $8.7$~dB at $256$, and $32$~dB at $512$, where the dark
rings are nulls. As with SAR, a denser fan or a lower bandwidth removes the rings: raising $R_h$
from $550$ to $2200$ at $B_s = 512$ brings the standard deviation back to $1.0$~dB.

\subsection{Side-Scan Sonar Beam Width}

In Figure \ref{fig:ss-beam-width}, we vary the azimuth beam width $\beta$ of the side-scan sonar of
Appendix \ref{sec:side-scan} logarithmically from $0.1^\circ$ to $10^\circ$, with the other
parameters at their defaults (Table \ref{tab:sss-params}) except the number of azimuth rays. The
platform passes the car of Figure \ref{fig:fls-beam-width} from a steeper pose, at a range of
$\lVert\bar{\vec{v}}\rVert = 7.5$ and an elevation of $e = 59.3^\circ$. The $P = 128$ pings are
spaced over a track of length $L = 1.2$, and each $128 \times 128$ image covers
$S_\ell = S_\rho = 1.2$. Each ping casts a fan of $R_h \times R_w = 550 \times 250$ rays over
$\Theta_h = 23^\circ$ in elevation and $\Theta_w = 3\beta$ in azimuth, in place of the default single
boresight ray, which would leave the beam nothing to weight. The $250$ azimuth rays are enough for
the image to converge even at $10^\circ$, where they are spread the most sparsely. The pulse is
Gaussian with $B_s = 30$, there is no time-varying gain, spreading, or absorption, and no
wavelength is given, so the returns add without their carrier phase. Each panel is shown in dB
relative to its own peak, with a floor of $-60$~dB.

At the range of the car, $\rho \approx 7.5$, a $0.1^\circ$ beam covers $\rho\beta \approx 0.013$ of
the track, about $1.4$ pixels, and the image resolves the outline of the car. The returns of the car
lie at near range, and behind them the car casts an acoustic shadow in which the seafloor returns
almost nothing. As the beam widens, each return is smeared along the track over $\rho\beta$ while
its range is unchanged. The ends of the car soften by $1^\circ$, where $\rho\beta \approx 0.13$, and
at $3.16^\circ$ the beam covers a third of the image and the car stretches into streaks across it.
At $10^\circ$, $\rho\beta \approx 1.3$ exceeds the width of the image, so every ping hears nearly
the whole car and each row is nearly constant: across the rows of the car and its shadow, the
standard deviation of a row averages $14\%$ of its mean, against $102\%$ at $0.1^\circ$. The streaks
stay at the same ranges as the car in the $0.1^\circ$ panel, since range resolution is set by $B_s$
rather than $\beta$. The shadow fills in as well, since a ping whose beam reaches past the ends of
the car hears unshadowed seafloor at the ranges of the shadow. Its mean amplitude rises from
$0.4\%$ of the seafloor beside it at $0.1^\circ$ to $28\%$ at $3.16^\circ$ and $69\%$ at $10^\circ$.

the track, so the averaging leaves it unchanged, and its median amplitude agrees to within about
$1\%$ across the panels. Relative to the falling peak, it brightens into the gray background of the

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we presented an open-source, GPU-accelerated SAR and sonar simulator implemented
in PyTorch that supports arbitrary 3D \texttt{.obj} models, multi-bounce ray tracing, and
configurable sensor parameters such as wavelength, bandwidth, sample rate, transmit waveform, and
material properties. From a single ray-traced signal model it renders four imaging modalities:
spotlight-mode SAR, stripmap-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, and forward-looking sonar. It addresses the
limitations of existing open-source tools such as \emph{RaySAR} and \emph{CVDomes}, which are each
constrained to a single SAR mode, do not simulate sonar, and rely on outdated software such as
MATLAB.

The proposed \emph{interposummation} technique enables efficient low-pass filtering during the
summation and interpolation of ray returns, accounting for the sensor's bandwidth and sampling
rate. Rather than range-compressing an already sampled signal, it evaluates the band-limited
response directly at the sample points from the off-grid ray returns, so interpolation and summation
happen in one step. This allows the simulator to generate physically meaningful signals without aliasing or
distortion due to improper interpolation. Interposummation extends to arbitrary transmit waveforms
by replacing its ideal sinc with the effective window of the waveform's matched filter, so sinc,
Gaussian, LFM chirp, Barker-13, or user-defined waveforms are simulated from the same ray trace at
the same cost, with the range sidelobes of a real pulse-compression system. When a carrier wavelength is given,
every return keeps its round-trip phase, and SAR images are formed by coherent integration of the
complex-valued signals, which we validated against ideal point scatterers and the physical-optics
glint of a flat plate.

We conducted a series of experiments on ShapeNet car models in spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan
sonar, and forward-looking sonar, while varying key parameters such as bandwidth, sampling rate, and beam width, with
further SAR examples varying the aperture, transmit waveform, wavelength, and sensor trajectory in
Appendix \ref{app:sar-examples}, and validated
the stripmap-mode reconstruction against spotlight mode. These experiments demonstrate how system
parameters influence image quality and signal fidelity. In particular, we observed in both SAR and
sonar that excessive oversampling of the energy-range scatter leads to poor signal quality and
numerical artifacts. This can be mitigated either by reducing the effective bandwidth, which lowers
the required sampling rate, or by increasing the ray density to maintain consistent energy
coverage across the target surface.
Future work will extend the simulator to support arbitrary flight paths and synthetic aperture
SONAR, broadening its applicability to a wider set of radar and sonar scenarios.

\section{Imaging Modalities}
\label{sec:modalities}

Any imaging algorithm may be applied to the simulated signals, and this section describes the four
used in this work: forward-looking sonar, side-scan sonar, and spotlight-mode and stripmap SAR.
When a carrier wavelength $\lambda$ is specified, each return carries the round-trip phase of its
own path, and
the energy-range scatter of (\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) becomes complex,
\begin{equation}
    g(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}\delta(z-z_k)
    .
    \label{eq:coherent-scatters}
\end{equation}
Interposummation is unchanged by this, so the digital signal of the $i^{th}$ pulse, sampled on the
one-way range axis, is
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{j 4 \pi \zeta_k / \lambda}w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:coherent-signal}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_k = z_k/2$ is the one-way range of the $k^{th}$ return.

\subsection{Forward-Looking Sonar}
\label{sec:forward-looking}

A forward-looking sonar image is formed from $P$ pings, the sonar term for pulses, all transmitted
from a single position with the beam steered to a different azimuth for each. The sensor sits at
$\vec{v}$, given by an azimuth $a$ and elevation $e$ as in Section \ref{sec:problem}, and its
boresight points at the scene origin. Range is measured from the delay of each return, and azimuth
is measured by steering the beam pattern of the sensor. Because every quantity in our simulator is
expressed in spatial units, the same model applies to sonar and radar alike.

Since the sensor is a point rather than a plane, the parallel $W_g \times H_g$ ray grid of the
signal simulation is replaced by a fan of $R_h \times R_w$ rays that all leave $\vec{v}$. Let
$\vec{u}_f = -\vec{v}/\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$ be the boresight direction, and let $\vec{u}_h$ and
$\vec{u}_v$ be the horizontal and vertical axes of the sensor. A ray launched at an azimuth
offset $\phi$ and an elevation offset $\psi$ from boresight has direction
\begin{equation}
    \vec{d}(\phi,\psi) = \cos(\psi)\cos(\phi)\,\vec{u}_f + \cos(\psi)\sin(\phi)\,\vec{u}_h + \sin(\psi)\,\vec{u}_v
    ,
    \label{eq:ray-fan}
\end{equation}
where $R_w$ values of $\phi$ are spaced uniformly over $[-\Theta_w/2,\Theta_w/2]$ and $R_h$ values
of $\psi$ over $[-\Theta_h/2,\Theta_h/2]$, and $\Theta_w \times \Theta_h$ is the angular field of
view of the fan. Parameterizing the fan by angle rather than by a flat image plane keeps the
angular sampling uniform, which matters because the beam pattern is applied in angle.

Ray tracing the fan yields the same energy-range pairs $(E_k,z_k)$ as before, with $z_k$ now the
true round-trip distance from $\vec{v}$. A point source loses energy over the surface of an
expanding sphere, which a planar wavefront does not, so each return is attenuated by the spreading
loss over its round-trip range. With the carrier
phase of (\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}), the energy of the $k^{th}$ return is
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{E}_k = \frac{E_k}{4 \pi z_k^2}e^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:sonar-energy}
\end{equation}
and the phasor is omitted when no wavelength is given.

Each return also carries two azimuths off boresight. Its transmit azimuth $\phi^{tx}_k$ is the
launch azimuth $\phi$ of the ray that produced it. Its receive azimuth $\phi^{rx}_k$ is the
azimuth of the point $\vec{p}_k$ that scattered it back to the sensor,
\begin{equation}
    \phi^{rx}_k = \operatorname{atan2}\big(\vec{u}_h\cdot(\vec{p}_k-\vec{v}),\,\vec{u}_f\cdot(\vec{p}_k-\vec{v})\big)
    .
    \label{eq:rx-azimuth}
\end{equation}
The two are equal for a first-bounce return. For a second-bounce return, $\phi^{tx}_k$ is the
direction of the ray's first path, while $\phi^{rx}_k$ is the direction of its second point of
contact, which is where a real receiver hears the energy arrive from.

The sensor cannot resolve the bearing of a single return, so each return is weighted by the beam
pattern on both transmit and receive. We model the one-way beam as a Gaussian with full width at
half maximum $\sqrt{2}\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    b(\phi) = \exp\bigg(-\frac{\phi^2}{2\sigma_\beta^2}\bigg)
    , \quad
    \sigma_\beta = \frac{\sqrt{2}\beta}{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}}
    ,
    \label{eq:beam-pattern}
\end{equation}
and weight the $k^{th}$ return by $b(\phi^{tx}_k)\,b(\phi^{rx}_k)$. For a first-bounce return this
product is a Gaussian with full width at half maximum $\beta$, the two-way beam width. The pattern
peaks at $1$ rather than integrating to $1$, so a return on boresight keeps its full energy, and
widening the beam spreads a return over more pings without dividing its energy among them.

Ping $i$ steers both beams to an azimuth $\phi_i$, where the $P$ values of $\phi_i$ are spaced
uniformly over $[-\Theta_p/2,\Theta_p/2]$. Steering shifts both patterns by $\phi_i$, and the
digital signal of the $i^{th}$ ping is the interposummation of the weighted returns,
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{E}_k\,b\big(\phi^{tx}_k-\phi_i\big)\,b\big(\phi^{rx}_k-\phi_i\big)\,w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:fls-signal}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_k = z_k/2$ is the one-way range of the $k^{th}$ return. The beam is applied after ray
tracing rather than during it, and the sensor does not move between pings, so every ping shares
the same returns and differs only in their weights. The fan is therefore ray traced once, and the
$P$ signals cost $P$ interposummations but a single ray trace.

The image has $M$ rows at one-way ranges $\rho_m$, spaced uniformly from $\rho_{near}$ in the
bottom row to $\rho_{far}$ in the top row, and one column per ping, with $\phi_i$ increasing from
left to right. The pixel at range $\rho_m$ and azimuth $\phi_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    I(\rho_m,\phi_i) = \big|s_i(\rho_m)\big|
    ,
    \label{eq:fls-image}
\end{equation}
where $|s_i|$ is evaluated between its samples by linear interpolation, and every signal is
sampled over a window that covers $[\rho_{near},\rho_{far}]$. The rows span a range swath
$S_\rho$ that brackets the scene origin along the line of sight, $\rho_{near} =
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert - S_\rho/2$ and $\rho_{far} = \lVert\vec{v}\rVert + S_\rho/2$, so the window
follows the sensor when it is moved away from the scene.

In range, the resolution of (\ref{eq:fls-signal}) is set by the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of $w(z)$,
as in SAR, rather than by the spacing of the rows. In azimuth, it is set by the beam width $\beta$
rather than by a synthetic aperture, and the pings sample it every $\Theta_p/(P-1)$. The azimuth
span of the image $\Theta_p$ is separate from the field of view $\Theta_w$ of the fan, and should
be narrower than it by about $3\beta$. The two-way beam falls to about $0.002$ at $1.5\beta$ off
its axis, so this margin puts rays under the whole beam of every ping, including the pings at the
edges of the image. A fan narrower than that leaves the outer pings with part of their beam unlit,
and the edges of the image darken. When $\lambda$ is given, the returns sum coherently within each
ping, so interference between nearby rays appears as speckle.

\subsection{Side-Scan Sonar}
\label{sec:side-scan}

A side-scan sonar image is formed from $P$ pings transmitted along a straight track, with a beam
that is never steered. The platform flies through a mean position $\bar{\vec{v}}$, given by $a$
and $e$ as in Section \ref{sec:problem}, and every ping looks in the same direction, the boresight
$\vec{u}_f = -\bar{\vec{v}}/\lVert\bar{\vec{v}}\rVert$ at the center of the track. The track runs
along the horizontal axis of the sensor, $\vec{u}_h = (\vec{u}_f \times \vec{u}_z)/\lVert\vec{u}_f
\times \vec{u}_z\rVert$, where $\vec{u}_z$ is the unit vector along the $z$ axis, so the scene
stays off to one side of the platform the whole way. Ping $i$ is transmitted from
\begin{equation}
    \vec{v}_i = \bar{\vec{v}} + \ell_i\vec{u}_h
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-track}
\end{equation}
where the $P$ offsets $\ell_i$ are spaced uniformly over $[-L/2,L/2]$ for a track of length $L$.
Where a forward-looking sonar sweeps its beam across the target from one position, a side-scan
sonar holds its beam fixed, and the target drifts across the beam as the platform passes.

Each ping casts its own fan (\ref{eq:ray-fan}) from $\vec{v}_i$, and every fan shares the axes
$\vec{u}_f$, $\vec{u}_h$ and $\vec{u}_v = \vec{u}_h \times \vec{u}_f$. The fan spans $\Theta_w =
3\beta$ in azimuth, since the two-way beam has fallen to about $0.002$ at its edges, and
$\Theta_h$ in elevation, which sets the swath of seafloor that is imaged. The returns take the
energy of (\ref{eq:sonar-energy}) with an added absorption loss over their round trip through the
water,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{E}_k = \frac{E_k e^{-\kappa z_k}}{4 \pi z_k^2}e^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-energy}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the absorption coefficient in nepers per $\ell$. The spreading loss
$4 \pi z_k^2$ can be disabled by setting \texttt{spherical\_spread} to False in the side-scan
configuration, as in Table \ref{tab:sss-params}. Their azimuths
$\phi^{tx}_k$ and $\phi^{rx}_k$ are measured off the fixed boresight as in (\ref{eq:rx-azimuth}),
with $\vec{v}_i$ in place of $\vec{v}$, so the azimuth of a return locates it along the track
rather than always lying near $0$.

The beam pattern (\ref{eq:beam-pattern}) is applied as in forward-looking sonar, but without
steering, and the digital signal of the $i^{th}$ ping is
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = z_o^{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{E}_k\,b\big(\phi^{tx}_k\big)\,b\big(\phi^{rx}_k\big)\,w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-signal}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the returns of that ping. The factor $z_o^{\eta}$ is a time-varying gain, a
receiver ramp against the fall of the seafloor return with range, and $\eta = 0$ disables it.
Every ping is sampled over the same window, which covers the range swath of the image, so the
samples of all pings line up in range.

Stacked as columns, the magnitudes $|s_i(z_o)|$ sample the scene over along-track offset $\ell_i$
and slant range $z_o$. The image interpolates them bilinearly onto an $H \times W$ pixel grid that
spans a cross-range swath $S_\ell$ along the track and a range swath $S_\rho$ in slant range,
centered on $\ell = 0$ and on the target range $\lVert\bar{\vec{v}}\rVert$, with near range in the
bottom row. Pixels outside the sampled track are zero, so the track length $L$ should be at least
$S_\ell$. The rows are in slant range, since no projection onto the ground plane is applied.

Along the track, the resolution is set by the beam: at slant range $\rho$ the beam covers about
$\rho\beta$ of the track, with $\beta$ in radians, so a narrower beam resolves finer detail. In
range, it is set by $B_s$ as before. The seafloor is seen at a grazing angle, so an object standing
off it occludes the ground down range and casts an acoustic shadow, and the top of the object
returns before the seafloor beneath it because it is closer to the sensor.

\subsection{Spotlight-Mode SAR}
\label{sec:spotlight-sar}

A spotlight-mode SAR image is formed by convolution backprojection \cite{spotlightmodesar}. The radar
transmits $P$ pulses from positions $\vec{v}_i$, at azimuth $a_i$ and elevation $e_i$, and every
pulse is aimed at the scene origin. The wavefront of the signal simulation is planar, so the
iso-range surfaces of pulse $i$ are planes normal to its look direction $\vec{u}_i =
-\vec{v}_i/\lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert$, and a point $\vec{x}$ on the ground plane returns at the
one-way range
\begin{equation}
    z_o = \lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert + \vec{u}_i\cdot\vec{x}
    .
    \label{eq:plane-wave-range}
\end{equation}
Only the horizontal part of $\vec{u}_i$ contributes to (\ref{eq:plane-wave-range}), and its length
is $\cos e_i$. Letting $\vec{n}_i$ be its unit vector, the ground direction of pulse $i$, the
sample at range $z_o$ comes from the ground line $\vec{n}_i\cdot\vec{x} = r$, where
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{z_o-\lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert}{\cos e_i}
    .
    \label{eq:ground-projection}
\end{equation}
Each pulse is therefore a projection of the scene reflectivity onto the line through the origin
along $\vec{n}_i$, sampled at the ground-plane rate $F_s\cos e_i$, and the image is recovered from
the $P$ projections as in computed tomography. The projection is undefined at $e_i = 90^\circ$,
where the pulse looks straight down and has no ground direction.

When a wavelength is given, each pulse is first demodulated,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}_i(z) = s_i(z)e^{-j 4 \pi z / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:demod}
\end{equation}
which removes the carrier of (\ref{eq:coherent-signal}) and integrates the pulses coherently.
Without a wavelength the signals are real and this step is skipped. Expressed in the ground
coordinate $r$ of (\ref{eq:ground-projection}), each pulse is filtered by weighting its spectrum
$\tilde{S}_i(Z)$ by the ramp $|Z|$,
\begin{equation}
    \check{s}_i(r) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|Z|\tilde{S}_i(Z)e^{j 2 \pi r Z}dZ
    ,
    \label{eq:cbp-ramp}
\end{equation}
and the filtered pulses are backprojected along their ground lines,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\gamma}(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\check{s}_i\big(\vec{n}_i\cdot\vec{x}\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:cbp}
\end{equation}
where $\check{s}_i$ is evaluated between its samples by linear interpolation, or by sinc
interpolation at the rate $F_s\cos e_i$. The ramp is the Jacobian of the polar-to-Cartesian change
of variables in the image's spatial-frequency plane, and the factor $1/4\pi^2$ comes from the
inverse two-dimensional Fourier transform in polar coordinates \cite{spotlightmodesar}. The image
samples $\vec{x}$ on an $H \times W$ grid of the ground plane centered on the origin, rotated so
that the radar illuminates it from the bottom edge, and the displayed image is
$|\hat{\gamma}(\vec{x})|$.

\subsection{Stripmap SAR}
\label{sec:stripmap-sar}

We describe the stripmap reconstruction used in this work, and in particular the filtering step
it requires. Let $\vec{v}_i$ be the sensor position on pulse $i$, and let $\rho_i(\vec{x}) =
\lVert\vec{v}_i-\vec{x}\rVert$ be the one-way range from that position to a ground point
$\vec{x}$. A direct estimate of the ground reflectivity $\gamma$ evaluates every pulse at the
range of $\vec{x}$, counter-rotates the carrier phase of that range, and averages,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\gamma}(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}s_i\big(\rho_i(\vec{x})\big)e^{-j 4 \pi \rho_i(\vec{x}) / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:naive-bp}
\end{equation}
where $s_i$ is evaluated between its samples by band-limited interpolation,
\begin{equation}
    s_i(\rho) = \sum_{n}s_i[n]\sinc\big(F_s(\rho-z_{i,n})\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:bp-interp}
\end{equation}
and $z_{i,n}$ is the range of the $n^{th}$ sample of pulse $i$.

Equation (\ref{eq:naive-bp}) is unfiltered backprojection, and it does not recover $\gamma$. Each
pulse spreads its range profile uniformly along its iso-range contours, which are straight lines
across the scene. A line is constant in the cross-range direction, so it deposits all of its
energy at the origin of the image's two-dimensional spatial-frequency plane, and averaging $P$
pulses stacks $P$ such contributions there. The reconstruction is then the laminogram familiar
from tomography: the true reflectivity convolved with $1/\lVert\vec{x}\rVert$, whose transfer
function is the reciprocal of the radial spatial frequency. Low spatial frequencies are amplified
without bound, and the scene is buried under a smooth pedestal.

Filtered backprojection suppresses the pedestal by weighting each pulse's spectrum with the
Jacobian of the polar-to-Cartesian change of variables, $dZ_xdZ_y = |Z|dZda$, which is the same
ramp filter as in the spotlight-mode reconstruction (\ref{eq:cbp-ramp}). What is specific to the
coherent case is where the null of that ramp belongs. The phasor in (\ref{eq:coherent-signal}) shifts the reflectivity spectrum up by $2/\lambda$, so the origin of
the image's spectrum lies at the carrier $Z = 2/\lambda$ rather than at $Z=0$, and nulling
baseband instead leaves most of the pile-up in place. We therefore demodulate each pulse with
(\ref{eq:demod}) before filtering, which slides the carrier onto $Z=0$, weight the spectrum
$\tilde{S}_i(Z)$ of $\tilde{s}_i(z)$ by $|Z|$,
\begin{equation}
    \check{s}_i(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|Z|\tilde{S}_i(Z)e^{j 2 \pi z Z}dZ
    ,
    \label{eq:ramp}
\end{equation}
and backproject the filtered pulses,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\gamma}(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\check{s}_i\big(\rho_i(\vec{x})\big)
    .
    \label{eq:filtered-bp}
\end{equation}
The counter-rotation of (\ref{eq:naive-bp}) does not reappear in (\ref{eq:filtered-bp}), because
(\ref{eq:demod}) has already removed the carrier. Weighting the undemodulated spectrum by
$|Z-2/\lambda|$ is equivalent. The displayed image is $|\hat{\gamma}(\vec{x})|$.

Over a linear trajectory with the azimuth spread swept from $0^\circ$ to $135^\circ$, replacing
(\ref{eq:naive-bp}) with (\ref{eq:filtered-bp}) raises the dynamic range of the reconstruction
from 18--29~dB to 59--93~dB and lowers its median-to-peak amplitude ratio from $0.12$--$0.29$ to
$0.02$--$0.04$. The filtered stripmap reconstruction and the spotlight-mode reconstruction of the
same signals agree to within $1$~dB of dynamic range. The far-field approximation $\rho_i(\vec{x})
\approx \lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert + \vec{u}_i\cdot\vec{x}$ of (\ref{eq:plane-wave-range}) may be substituted
for the exact range in (\ref{eq:naive-bp}) and (\ref{eq:filtered-bp}), and affects neither the
pedestal nor the analysis above.

\section{SAR Image Examples}
\label{app:sar-examples}
This appendix shows spotlight-mode SAR images of the car of Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.a as single parameters
are varied from the defaults of Table \ref{tab:params}: the aperture, the noise, the wavelength, the
transmit waveform, and the sensor trajectory.

\subsection{Aperture, Noise, and Wavelength}

In Figure \ref{fig:az-spread} we showcase our simulation when the range of azimuth angles (azimuth spread) is varied for the spotlight-mode SAR algorithm. With little azimuth spread, there is not much detail in the SAR image. At a $360^\circ$ spread the entire car is surrounded by the aperture, and a second set of fainter lines appears within the outline of the car due to the second bounce of the rays.
        
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_az_spread.png}
  \caption{Varying the azimuth spread in our simulation.}
  \label{fig:az-spread}
\end{figure}

In Figure \ref{fig:num-pulse}, we illustrate the effect of varying the number of transmitted pulses in our simulation. With a small number of pulses, the reconstructed image appears coarse and lacks definition. As the pulse count increases, the image quality improves noticeably, revealing sharper boundaries and more distinct structural features of the target.
        
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_num_pulse.png}
  \caption{Effect of varying the number of pulses in our simulation.}
  \label{fig:num-pulse}
\end{figure}

In Figure \ref{fig:snr}, we demonstrate how varying the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in decibels (dB) influences the quality of the simulated SAR image. In real SAR scenarios SNR decreases as range to the target increases. At low SNR values (around 0 dB), the image is dominated by noise, obscuring most structural details. As the SNR increases toward 22 dB, the object becomes clearly resolved, with well-defined features. SNR is calculated as an average over all signals used to generate the spotlight-mode SAR image.
        
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_snrdb.png}
  \caption{Varying the SNR in our simulation.}
  \label{fig:snr}
\end{figure}

In Figure \ref{fig:wavelength}, we analyze the effect of varying the wavelength parameter on the spotlight-mode SAR reconstruction. We use coherent integration of the complex-valued signals in both spotlight-mode and stripmap-mode SAR. Image quality is clearest at wavelengths that are well matched to the range resolution of the simulated system; wavelengths that are much shorter than the ray-grid spacing introduce interference patterns from constructive and destructive summation across nearby rays.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_wavelength.png}
  \caption{Varying the wavelength in our simulation with coherent integration.}
  \label{fig:wavelength}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Transmit Waveform}

In Figure \ref{fig:waveform}, we vary the transmit waveform, which replaces the
ideal sinc of interposummation with the effective window $w(z)$ of the
corresponding pulse-compression matched filter as described in Section
\ref{sec:waveforms} and illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}.
The ray trace is identical across the panels, and only the effective window
changes. We
compare an ideal sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, and a Barker-13 phase
code, all designed for the same spatial bandwidth $B_s$. The sinc, LFM chirp,
and Barker-13 reconstructions share a comparable range resolution but are
accompanied by the range sidelobes characteristic of each waveform, which
appear as faint replicas alongside the strong scatterers. The Gaussian pulse
produces no range sidelobes, but it does a poor job of suppressing
out-of-bandwidth frequencies, leaving much of its energy outside the allotted
band $\pm B_s/2$. Its gradual roll-off also tapers the in-band response, which
costs coherent gain and yields the visibly dimmer reconstruction in Figure
\ref{fig:waveform}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_waveform.png}
  \caption{Varying the transmit waveform in our simulation. Each panel uses the effective interposummation window $w(z)$ of the labeled pulse-compression waveform, all with the same spatial bandwidth $B_s$.}
  \label{fig:waveform}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Sensor Trajectory}

In Figure \ref{fig:trajectory-type}, we compare the two supported sensor trajectories. The circular trajectory sweeps the sensor along a constant-radius arc around the target, while the linear trajectory translates the sensor along a straight path. The circular trajectory produces a more uniformly illuminated image because the range from the sensor to the target is nearly constant across pulses. The linear trajectory is the collection geometry of stripmap-mode SAR. Both are imaged here by convolution backprojection, and Appendix \ref{sec:stripmap-sar} shows that the stripmap-mode reconstruction of the same linear-trajectory signals matches it to within $1$~dB of dynamic range.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim=0 0 0 0, clip, width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/sar_stitched_trajectory_type.png}
  \caption{Comparison of circular and linear sensor trajectories in our simulation.}
  \label{fig:trajectory-type}
\end{figure}

In Figure \ref{fig:trajectory-noise}, we analyze the impact of imperfect knowledge of the sensor trajectory. We add zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance $\sigma^2$ to the perceived sensor position while the true trajectory used for signal simulation is kept noise-free. This models the case where the reconstruction algorithm has an imprecise estimate of the sensor pose. As the noise variance increases, the reconstructed image becomes progressively blurred as the pulses no longer coherently integrate at the correct locations.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
The experimental figures and tables are produced by scripts in the code repository, each run with no arguments.
The scripts read their defaults from \texttt{config.json} (Appendix \ref{app:params}), whose \texttt{srn\_cars\_dir} points to the srncars dataset \cite{srn}, and the conda environment is given in \texttt{environment.yml}. For example:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}: \texttt{sar\_overview\_figure.py}
  \item Figures \ref{fig:sphere-size}, \ref{fig:fsbw}, and \ref{fig:az-spread}--\ref{fig:trajectory-noise}: \texttt{sar\_paper\_figures.py}
  \item Figures \ref{fig:fls-beam-width}--\ref{fig:ss-fsbw}: \texttt{fls\_paper\_figures.py} and \texttt{sss\_paper\_figures.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:validation}: \texttt{sar\_validation.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:materials}: \texttt{convert\_cvdomes.py}, then \texttt{render\_cvdomes.py}
  \item Table \ref{tab:compute-time}: \texttt{compute\_time\_experiment.py}
\end{itemize}
