\Title{Open-Source SAR and Sonar Simulator in PyTorch Via Accumulation and Interpolation of Ray Returns}

\Author{Alex Berian *, Sehajdeep Singh Bal, JhihYang Wu, Daniel Brignac and Abhijit Mahalanobis}

\AuthorNames{Alex Berian, Sehajdeep Singh Bal, JhihYang Wu, Daniel Brignac and Abhijit Mahalanobis}

\address[1]{%
Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, The University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, USA; berian@arizona.edu (A.B.); sehajdeepbal@arizona.edu (S.S.B.); jhihyangwu@arizona.edu (J.W.); dbrignac@arizona.edu (D.B.); amahalan@arizona.edu (A.M.)}

\corres{Correspondence: berian@arizona.edu}

\abstract{There is currently a lack of open-source SAR and sonar simulators. \emph{RaySAR} simulates signals from ray-traced contributions but does not model a transmit waveform, and depends on outdated software such as POV-Ray and MATLAB, while \emph{CVDomes} is a fixed dataset of ten vehicles whose simulation code is not public. Neither simulates sonar. We present an \emph{open-source SAR and sonar simulator} implemented in \emph{PyTorch} with \emph{GPU acceleration} that renders three imaging modalities from a single ray-traced signal model: \emph{spotlight-mode SAR}, \emph{side-scan sonar}, and \emph{forward-looking sonar}. It supports arbitrary \texttt{.obj} 3D models, with configurable wavelength, sample rate, bandwidth, transmit waveform, and material properties. We simulate multi-bounce ray tracing to model realistic reflectivity, scattering, and absorption. We introduce an efficient low-pass filtering technique that \emph{accumulates and interpolates} ray returns onto the sample grid of the sensor in a single step, accounting for its effective bandwidth and sample rate. Each return keeps the round-trip phase of its path, so the simulated signals are complex-valued and support \emph{coherent imaging}. This accumulation and interpolation accepts \emph{arbitrary transmit waveforms} through the effective window of their matched filter, so pulse-compression waveforms such as LFM chirps and Barker codes are simulated from the same ray trace as an ideal band-limited pulse. The simulator further supports radar cross section modeling, different noise types, and imperfect sensor trajectories. We test our simulator on ShapeNet car models with spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, and forward-looking sonar.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

SAR and sonar simulators are crucial for developing and validating radar and sonar algorithms when real-world data is unavailable or not feasible to process. By providing controlled, repeatable environments, simulators allow researchers to test imaging techniques, evaluate performance under different conditions, and benchmark novel methods without requiring extensive field experiments.

Most powerful simulators, such as XPatch \cite{xpatch4}, are either classified in some manner or proprietary to their respective developer. The openly available alternatives lack flexibility: CVDomes \cite{cvdomes} is a fixed dataset of ten vehicles whose simulation code is not public, RaySAR \cite{raysar} simulates signals from ray-traced contributions but does not model a transmit waveform and depends on outdated POV-Ray and MATLAB software, and neither simulates sonar. XPatch and the simulation behind CVDomes compute scattering with the shooting-and-bouncing-rays (SBR) method \cite{sbr}, which launches a dense grid of rays at the target and traces their reflections, and our ray tracer follows the same ray-launching approach.

To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first scalable PyTorch-based SAR and sonar simulation framework that allows for any 3D \texttt{.obj} model. Our simulator allows users to specify sample rate, bandwidth, transmit waveform, material properties (reflectivity, scatter, absorption), and sensor trajectory.
The primary functionality of the simulator is to use ray tracing combined with a band-limited \emph{accumulation and interpolation} of the ray returns to simulate signals that correspond to the sensor's wavelength, bandwidth, sample rate, and transmit waveform. The number of bounces traced for each ray is configurable. When a carrier wavelength is given, every ray return carries its round-trip phase, so the signals are complex-valued and SAR images are formed by coherent integration. Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accumulation and interpolation of ray returns}, a low-pass filter that produces signals matched to the bandwidth and sample rate of the sensor and preserves the carrier phase of every return for coherent imaging. Unlike standard range compression, which filters a signal already sampled on a uniform grid, it evaluates the band-limited response directly from irregularly spaced ray returns, so one step both interpolates each return onto the sample grid and accumulates all returns into each sample.
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary transmit waveforms.} Any transmit waveform enters the simulation through the effective window of its matched filter, so an ideal sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, a Barker-13 code, or any other sampled waveform is simulated from the same ray trace, with the range sidelobes of the corresponding pulse-compression system. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the only publicly available SAR or sonar simulator that supports arbitrary transmit waveforms.
    \item \textbf{Three imaging modalities} from one simulator: spotlight-mode SAR \cite{spotlightmodesar}, side-scan sonar (SSS), and forward-looking sonar (FLS). Every quantity in the simulator is expressed in spatial units, so radar and sonar share the same ray tracer, material model, and accumulation and interpolation of returns, and differ only in how rays are launched, how returns are weighted by the beam and the medium, and how the signals are formed into an image.
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:modality-comparison} highlights this flexibility, rendering a spotlight-mode SAR image by convolution backprojection (CBP), an SSS image, and an FLS image of four different ShapeNet car models with the same simulator.

Figure \ref{fig:main-fig} showcases examples of what our simulator produces for a ShapeNet \cite{shapenet} car model: a spotlight-mode SAR image, together with the depth map, energy map, energy-range scatter plot, and received signal of a single pulse.

The overall process of our signal simulation is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim}. Our simulation
functions by simulating a grid of parallel rays with equal energy. The rays hit a triangle on the
\texttt{.obj} mesh. When a ray hits the mesh, some of the energy is reflected, some energy is
scattered back to the sensor, and some energy is lost to absorption. The reflected energy continues
along the next path of the ray, which once again hits another triangle on the mesh with reflectivity,
scattering, and absorption, up to the configured number of bounces. We gather the scattered energy and its
corresponding traveled range into an energy-range scatter plot. From the energy-range scatter plot we use a sinc pulse, with width
corresponding to the radar system bandwidth, to accumulate and interpolate the
scatters into each sample of the received signal. The spacing
between each sinc pulse corresponds to the sample rate of the radar system. Unlike conventional
filtering of a sampled signal, this single step places every off-grid return at its exact range and
sums it with its neighbors, without first binning the returns onto a discrete range grid. Once there is a
received signal for each pulse at different azimuth and elevation angles, further analysis or
imaging can be done.

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}

Most high-fidelity radar signature codes build on the shooting-and-bouncing-rays (SBR) method
\cite{sbr}, and the best known of them, XPatch \cite{xpatch4}, is not publicly available.
Openly available simulated data of vehicles comes mainly from two sources: CVDomes
\cite{cvdomes}, a precomputed dataset of simulated phase histories, and RaySAR \cite{raysar}, a
ray-tracing simulator of SAR signals and images. Neither covers sonar, and neither lets the user choose the
transmit waveform.

\subsection{Shooting and Bouncing Rays}
\label{sec:sbr}

Ling, Chou, and Lee introduced SBR \cite{sbr} to compute the radar cross section (RCS) of
arbitrarily shaped cavities, such as jet engine inlets, whose interior reflections are too
numerous for closed-form solutions and too electrically large for full-wave solvers. The incident
plane wave is represented by a dense grid of parallel rays shot into the opening of the cavity.
Each ray is traced by geometrical optics as it bounces off the cavity walls, carrying its
amplitude, phase, and polarization along its path until it leaves through the opening, and the
scattered far field is then obtained by a physical-optics integration of the exiting ray fields
over the opening. Since the ray grid only needs to be dense enough to sample the illuminated
surface, the method scales to large, geometrically complex targets, and it became the core of
high-frequency codes such as XPatch \cite{xpatch4} and the code behind CVDomes. Our ray tracer
launches its rays the same way, as a grid of rays traced through multiple bounces. It differs in
what each ray carries: rather than a polarized field integrated by physical optics frequency by
frequency, each return keeps only its energy and total path length (Section
\ref{sec:signal-simulation}), and the sampled signal at the sensor's bandwidth is formed by accumulating
and interpolating these returns. This scalar formulation lets the same ray trace drive both radar and
sonar.

\subsection{CVDomes}
\label{sec:cvdomes}

CVDomes \cite{cvdomes,cvdomesreport} is a dataset of simulated signals rather than a public simulator: $369$~GB of simulated
X-band phase histories of ten civilian vehicle facet models, from a Toyota Camry to a Toyota
Tacoma pickup, distributed by the Air Force Research Laboratory as MATLAB files. It was computed
with the same proprietary high-frequency code as the earlier Backhoe dataset \cite{backhoe}, which
traces shooting and bouncing rays through up to four bounces at ten rays per wavelength to induce
surface currents, and integrates those currents with physical optics into the monostatic far
field. Each facet model was given an asphalt ground plane, since the double-bounce returns between
the ground and the vehicle dominate measured signatures, and per-facet material labels: a perfect
electric conductor for the body, a $0.25$-inch glass layer ($\epsilon_r = 5.9 - j0.15$) backed by
air so that no rays enter the unmodeled interior, absorber for rubber and all interior fill, and an
asphalt half-space ($\epsilon_r = 5.9 - j0.1$). The scattering is fully polarimetric (HH, HV, VV)
over $360^\circ$ of azimuth and $30^\circ$--$60^\circ$ of elevation, with $512$ frequency samples
spanning $5.35$~GHz about a center frequency of $9.6$~GHz, for a range resolution of about
$2.8$~cm. To make this affordable, rays were traced once per $0.5^\circ \times 0.5^\circ$ sector of
azimuth and elevation, and the fields across each sector were extrapolated from that single trace
with a local point-scatterer approximation, giving a $0.0625^\circ$ angular step and a $14.3$~m
unambiguous range. The extrapolation cut the run time about 75-fold, yet the dataset still took
over $72{,}000$ CPU hours on a $2048$-processor cluster. CVDomes is imaged with the far-field
backprojection code of the MATLAB SAR image formation toolbox \cite{gorham2010sar}, and because it
stores the whole dome of look angles, it supports 2D spotlight and circular SAR images as well as
multi-pass 3D reconstruction \cite{cvdomes}. Its limits are those of a fixed dataset: the ten
vehicles, the band, and the far-field monostatic geometry cannot be changed, and since the
electromagnetic code is not public, no new targets can be added. We reuse its Camry facet model
and material labels in Section \ref{sec:per-triangle-materials}.

\subsection{RaySAR}
\label{sec:raysar}

RaySAR \cite{raysar,auer2011thesis} is an open-source SAR simulator built on a modified version of
the POV-Ray ray tracer, with MATLAB scripts for image formation. It was developed for interpreting
high-resolution SAR images of complex urban scenes. The sensor is modeled as an orthographic camera
with a parallel light source at the same position, approximating a far-field plane wave. POV-Ray
traces rays through the scene up to a chosen number of bounces, and for every ray and bounce
writes a \emph{contribution}: its azimuth, slant range, and elevation coordinates, an intensity from
diffuse and specular reflection models, its bounce level, and a flag marking specular paths. The
MATLAB scripts bin these contributions into azimuth-range resolution cells to form reflectivity
maps, which can be separated by bounce level to show layover, foreshortening, shadowing, and
multiple-bounce effects, while the elevation coordinate supports 3D analysis of the scatterers.
RaySAR thus simulates the signal directly on the azimuth-range grid of the image, without a transmit
waveform: the resolution of the sensor enters only through the size of the resolution cells. A Python port \cite{raysarpython} re-implements the image formation
step to generate training data for automatic target recognition, while still relying on POV-Ray for
the contributions. It places each contribution in its azimuth-range cell with a phase of $-4\pi
r/\lambda$ at a fixed wavelength of $3.1$~cm, optionally perturbed at random, and sums the complex
values. The finite bandwidth is not modeled; instead, a star-shaped sidelobe pattern is imitated by
adding an inverse-square decay along the row and column of every pixel above a threshold, before
the image is clipped in dB and quantized to 8 bits. Both versions therefore simulate the signal
on the image grid without modeling a transmit waveform, whereas our simulator turns each ray's returns
into a band-limited, sampled, complex received signal for a user-chosen waveform and forms the image
from that signal.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
A \texttt{.obj} file is a collection of $V$ vertices in 3D space and $F$ triangle faces. Our goal
is to produce a SAR or sonar image of the mesh from $P$ pulses (pings, in sonar) of a sensor with a
given transmit waveform, bandwidth, and sample rate. A signal needs to be simulated for each pulse
from a known azimuth angle $a$ and elevation angle $e$. From the simulated signals, we form
spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, or forward-looking sonar images, or do
other analyses.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Signal Simulation}
\label{sec:signal-simulation}


The general process for simulating signals is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim}. We cast a grid of
$R_h \times R_w$ parallel rays that originate on a grid of size $W_g \times H_g$. The center of the
grid intersects a line from the origin for a given azimuth $a$ and elevation $e$ angle. 
Azimuth $a$ is defined as the angle from the $x$ to the $y$ axis, and elevation $e$ is the 
angle from the $x$-$y$ plane to the $z$ axis. The grid is orthogonal to this line defined by the 
azimuth/elevation. This set of rays can be viewed as a simulated wavefront. The size of the ray 
grid only needs to be big enough to cover the ground space desired for imaging.

When a ray makes contact with a surface, a fraction of its energy is reflected into the next path
of the ray, a fraction is absorbed, and a fraction is scattered. We model these three fractions as
$R,A,S$. Scattering is the only component that returns energy to the receiver. The fractions can be
assigned to each of the $F$ faces of the mesh by a \texttt{.mp} file that accompanies the
\texttt{.obj}, with one line of $(R,A,I,D,S)$ per triangle (Section \ref{sec:per-triangle-materials}).
Without one, we use only two materials, one for the object and one for the ground, whose values are
listed in Table \ref{tab:materials}.

The three fractions of a surface add up to 1, so that a surface never returns more energy than it
receives:
\begin{equation}
    R+S+A = 1
    .
\end{equation}
Only scattering is considered for returned energy. Scattering occurs in two ways, directional
intensity $I$ and diffuse scattering $D$, which split the scattered fraction:
\begin{equation}
    D + I = 1
    .
\end{equation}
Material properties that do not satisfy these constraints are divided by their sums before ray
tracing. Table \ref{tab:materials} gives the default materials in both forms. The car scatters about a
third of the incident energy, almost entirely in the directional lobe, so it returns strong glints
near the mirror direction and little elsewhere. The ground scatters weakly and mostly diffusely, so it
returns a dim, nearly uniform clutter that does not mask the car.

Returned energy from a particular ray $E_r$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Big(m_I\cos({\theta/2})^\alpha + m_D\Big)
\end{equation}
where $E_{in}$ is the ray energy going into the surface, $\theta$ is the angle between the 
direction of the reflected ray $\vec{u}_r$ and the direction to the line of sight $\vec{u}_l$,
$\alpha$ sets the width of the directional lobe, and $m_I,m_D$ are multipliers that conserve the
scattered energy.

We solve for the multipliers by ensuring that energy scattered in all directions integrates to
$SE_{in}$:
\begin{equation}
	S E_{in} = \oiint\limits_{H} S E_{in} \Big(m_I\cos({\theta/2})^\alpha + m_D\Big)d\Omega
\end{equation}
where $H$ denotes the hemisphere of directions from the surface.

\begin{equation}
    1 = m_I \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega + m_D \oiint\limits_{H} 1 d \Omega
\end{equation}

Now we can accurately ensure that the total amount of energy diffuse scattered is $D$ and the 
total amount of energy directionally scattered is $I$.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:directional-int}
    I = m_I \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega
    .
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    D =  m_D \oiint\limits_{H} 1 d \Omega = 2 \pi m_D
    .
\end{equation}

Using the half angle trigonometry identity,
\begin{equation}
    \cos\frac{\theta}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\cos \theta}{2}}
    ,
\end{equation}
and we can express $\cos\theta$ using dot product
\begin{equation}
    \cos\theta = \vec{u}_{l}\cdot \vec{u}_{r}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{u}_l$ is the line-of-sight vector which is the directional vector from the point of 
contact of the ray and where the radar is. $\vec{u}_r$ and other directional vectors are expanded 
as 
\begin{equation}
    \vec{u}_r = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \cos(a_r)\cos(e_r) \\
            \sin(a_r)\cos(e_r) \\
            \sin(e_r)
        \end{bmatrix}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $a_r$ and $e_r$ are the azimuth/elevation angles relative to the surface for the directional
vector $\vec{u}_r$. The integral in (\ref{eq:directional-int}) can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:big-int}
    \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega = \int_{e_l=0}^{e_l=\pi/2}\int_{a_l=0}^{a_l=2 \pi}\sqrt{\frac{1+\vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{u}_l}{2}}^\alpha \cos(e_l) da_lde_l
    .
\end{equation}
The integral in (\ref{eq:big-int}) is identical for any relative azimuth angle of the reflected
ray $a_r$, so it depends only on the relative elevation angle of the reflected ray $e_r$. It is
impractical to compute this integral for each ray hitting each surface on the object mesh, so we
seek a cheaper equivalent. Computing $e_r$ directly requires trigonometry, but a quantity that is
one-to-one with $e_r$ follows from a single dot product,
\begin{equation}
    \cos\bigg(\frac{\pi}{2}-e_r\bigg) = \vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{n}
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\vec{n}$ is the normal vector of the surface. While $\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n}$ is cheap to
evaluate, the integral itself remains expensive, so we approximate it with a polynomial
$f_{\alpha,poly}$ that is specific to $\alpha$,

\begin{equation}
    f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n}) \approx \oiint\limits_{H} \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha d \Omega
    .
\end{equation}

With the integrals computed, the multipliers are given by
\begin{equation}
    m_D = \frac{D}{2 \pi}
    ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    m_I = \frac{I}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})}
    ,
\end{equation}
which yields our final expression for returned energy as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:almost}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Bigg(\frac{I \cos({\theta/2})^\alpha}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})} + \frac{D}{2\pi}\Bigg)
\end{equation}
The $\theta / 2$ in the numerator of (\ref{eq:almost}) refers to the angle between the direction
back to the radar and the direction of the reflected ray, whereas the $\theta / 2$ in
(\ref{eq:big-int}) refers to the angle between the reflected ray direction and all possible
line-of-sight vectors.
Again, to avoid trigonometric computations for each ray, we substitute $\cos(\theta/2)$ to give
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:returned-energy}
    E_r = E_{in} S \Bigg(\frac{I \sqrt{\frac{1+\vec{u}_r \cdot \vec{u}_l}{2}}^\alpha}{f_{\alpha,poly}(\vec{u}_{r} \cdot \vec{n})} + \frac{D}{2\pi}\Bigg)
\end{equation}

The energy reflected into the next path of the ray after bouncing is given by
\begin{equation}
    E_{out} = RE_{in}
    ,
    \label{eq:reflected-energy}
\end{equation}
where $E_{out}$ is the energy carried into the next path of the ray after the bounce, and $R$ is
the reflectivity of the triangle in the object mesh that has been hit. The energy of the first
path of each ray is set to unity, and (\ref{eq:reflected-energy}) supplies $E_{in}$ for every path
thereafter. Every modality divides its returns by the number of transmitted rays $R_hR_w$, so the
sensor transmits a total energy of 1 (Section \ref{sec:units}). This is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:terminology}.
If the next path of a ray hits a location that is out of the line of sight, it is not considered
for returned energy. If a ray has no intersections, it is also not considered. The number of
bounces is set by \texttt{num\_bounce} (Tables \ref{tab:params}, \ref{tab:fls-params}, and \ref{tab:sss-params}), and each ray contributes at most one return per bounce.

  \centering

  \caption{Visual example of terminology for (\ref{eq:returned-energy}).}
  \label{fig:terminology}
\end{figure}

Ray tracing the full grid therefore yields a set of $N$ energy-range pairs $(E_k,z_k)$,
where $E_k$ is the \emph{return weight} of the $k^{th}$ return, its returned energy
(\ref{eq:returned-energy}) divided by $R_hR_w$, and
$z_k$ is the total distance traveled by that ray from and back to the ray grid plane. This
ray-launching stage follows standard SBR practice \cite{sbr}. Our contribution lies in how the
resulting returns are turned into a sampled signal, described next. We treat the
returned energy from ray tracing as a continuous spatial signal made up of Dirac
delta functions.
\begin{equation}
    g(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\delta(z-z_k)
    .
    \label{eq:returned-scatters}
\end{equation}
This set of energy-range pairs is called the energy-range scatter plot. The Fourier transform is
then given by
\begin{equation}
    G(Z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{- j 2 \pi z_k Z}
\end{equation}
which has infinite bandwidth, meaning it is impossible to sample without aliasing. We want to
interpolate and sum $g(z)$ with sufficiently low bandwidth and sample rate that we do not see
the jagged effects of the Dirac delta functions.

A radar system typically uses pulse-compression techniques to improve range resolution. In short,
the bandwidth of the received signal is approximately the bandwidth of the radar pulse used. The
temporal bandwidth and sample rate of the received signal are $B_t$ and $F_t$ respectively. They
are related to the \emph{spatial bandwidth} and \emph{spatial sample rate} as $B_s = 2B_t/c$ and
$F_s = 2F_t/c$, where $c$ is the propagation speed, $3\times10^8$~m/s for radar and about
$1500$~m/s for sound in water. The corresponding spatial sample
spacing is $T_s = 1/F_s$.

We propose to simultaneously interpolate and sum the received set of scatters in
(\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) using a sinc pulse train given by
\begin{equation}
    p(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sinc\big(B_s(z-nT_s)\big)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $\sinc(x) = \sin(\pi x)/\pi x$. The simulated digital signal evaluated at the range $z_o$ is
then given by
\begin{equation}
    s(z_o) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sinc\big(B_s(z-z_o)\big)\sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\delta \bigg(z-\frac{z_k}{2} \bigg) dz
    .
\end{equation}
We divide the scatter's range $z_k$ by $2$ because of the two-way travel, which aligns the
samples with their position in space. Then with the sifting property $s(z_o)$ is reduced to
\begin{equation}
    s(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_k\sinc\Bigg(B_s\bigg(z_o-\frac{z_k}{2}\bigg)\Bigg)
    .
    \label{eq:accum-interp}
\end{equation}
The $n^{th}$ sample of the resulting digital signal corresponds to the range $z=nT_s$. The sinc
function acts as both a low-pass interpolation filter and a summation over nearby samples in the
energy-range scatter. This differs from standard interpolation, which resamples a signal already
given on a uniform grid, and from standard range compression, which filters such a sampled signal.
Here the inputs are $N$ returns at arbitrary continuous ranges $z_k/2$, often many per sample
spacing, and (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) interpolates each of them onto the sample grid and
accumulates them in a single step. We therefore refer to (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) as the
\emph{accumulation and interpolation} of the returns. Since it is linear in $E_k$, it applies unchanged to complex returns that carry their
carrier phase (\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}), so the simulated signals preserve phase for coherent
imaging.


\subsection{Units and Scale}
\label{sec:units}

Every length in the simulator, including the mesh, the ray grid, the sensor distance, the
wavelength $\lambda$ and the range axis, is expressed in one arbitrary unit $\ell$, and $B_s$ and
$F_s$ are in cycles per $\ell$. The ShapeNet meshes are normalized so that a car is about
$0.9\ell$ long. Time never enters the simulation, so the propagation speed $c$ appears only when
converting $B_s$ and $F_s$ to temporal frequencies. The simulated signals depend only on ratios of
lengths, such as the range resolution $1/B_s$ to the size of the car or $\lambda$ to the ray
spacing, so a scene in $\ell$ maps to any physical scale. Table \ref{tab:units} converts the
defaults for a car about $4.5$~m long, or $1\ell \approx 5$~m.

The bandwidth corresponds to a high-resolution radar, and to a low-frequency sonar. The SAR
wavelength does not: an X-band radar at this scale has $\lambda \approx 3$~cm $\approx 0.006\ell$.
A short wavelength needs the ray spacing to sample its phase, $\Delta < \lambda/(4\sin\phi)$
(Section \ref{sec:validation}), or the returns of neighboring rays interfere at random within each
pulse. At the default $208 \times 208$ rays ($\Delta \approx 0.006\ell$) the images break down at
$\lambda = 0.02\ell$ and below (Figure \ref{fig:wavelength} of Appendix \ref{app:sar-examples}), and X-band would need about four
times the ray density along each axis, or $15$ times the rays. Coherent integration across pulses
would also need about $15$ times the $64$ default pulses to sample the aperture without aliasing.
We therefore use a wavelength much longer than a physical radar's for the same scene, which keeps
the phase well sampled at an affordable ray count.

Energy is also unitless. The sensor transmits a total energy of 1, split equally among its rays,
so a return weight $E_k$ is the fraction of the transmitted energy that the $k^{th}$ return brings
back to the sensor, and a signal or image value is a sum of such fractions. The coherent sum
(\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}) adds the return weights as field amplitudes, and $|s|^2$ is taken as
received power, as in the flat-plate validation of Section \ref{sec:rcs}. The physically
consistent amplitude would be $\sqrt{E_k}$. Using $E_k$ instead preserves where returns fall and
their phases, but squares their relative amplitudes, which doubles the dynamic range of the images
in dB. No antenna gain, transmit power or
receiver noise figure is modeled, so signal values are relative to the transmitted energy rather
than calibrated in watts.

\subsection{Arbitrary Transmit Waveforms}
\label{sec:waveforms}

The accumulation and interpolation in (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) simulates an ideal band-limited pulse.
Real radar and sonar systems instead transmit pulse-compression waveforms and match-filter the
echoes, and the range sidelobes of the waveform shape the final image. We therefore generalize
it to an arbitrary transmit waveform. We can express a received continuous signal $r(z)$ from our scatters model in
(\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) with an arbitrary transmitted waveform $x(z)$ as
\begin{equation}
    r(z) = x(z) * g(z)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $*$ is convolution. It is common in practical radar systems for the transmitted signal
$x(z)$ to be a pulse-compression waveform such as an LFM chirp or Barker-13. To compute the
recovered signal we use the matched filter, which is the time reverse and complex conjugate of
$x(z)$,
\begin{equation}
    h(z) = x^*(-z)
    .
\end{equation}
The received and recovered match-filtered digital signal $s_{mf}(z_o)$ evaluated at the range $z_o
$ is then given by 
\begin{equation}
    s_{mf}(z_o) = h(z)*r(z) |_{z=z_o}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    = h(z)*x(z)*g(z) |_{z=z_o}
     \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    =w(z)*g(z) |_{z=z_o}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    =\sum_{k=1}^{N}E_kw\bigg(z_o-\frac{z_k}{2}\bigg)
    ,
\end{equation}
where $w(z) = h(z) * x(z)$ is the effective windowing function, and $|_{z=z_o}$ means to evaluate the expression at $z=z_o$. Although $w(z)*g(z)$ has the form of conventional matched filtering, it is never computed by convolving a sampled received signal. Since $g(z)$ is a sum of off-grid delta functions, the matched-filter output is evaluated in closed form at each sample $z_o$, so the same sum performs both the interpolation and the summation of the returns. One may view the ideal band-limited digital signal in (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) as the case $w(z)=\sinc(B_s z)$, which has no frequency domain sidelobes.

Each transmit waveform therefore replaces the ideal sinc of (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) with a different effective window $w(z)$. Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows} compares the effective windows produced by a sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, and a Barker-13 code, all designed for the same spatial bandwidth $B_s$. In the range domain (Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}, left) the sinc yields the narrowest mainlobe but slowly decaying range sidelobes, while the Gaussian pulse trades a wider mainlobe for the complete suppression of sidelobes. The LFM chirp and Barker-13 waveforms, which are widely used in practical radar systems, achieve a mainlobe comparable to the sinc while exhibiting the characteristic range sidelobes of pulse compression.

In the frequency domain (Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}, right) the sinc window fills the band $\pm B_s/2$ with a sharp cutoff, whereas the Gaussian rolls off smoothly and the LFM and Barker-13 spectra fill the band with some ripple. Because the returns are accumulated and interpolated the same way for any $w(z)$, our simulator can substitute any of these effective windows to emulate the range response of a real radar's or sonar's pulse-compression waveform. The waveform is not limited to these four: any transmit waveform $x(z)$ sampled on a fine range grid is supported, since its effective window is computed once as the autocorrelation of $x(z)$ and then linearly interpolated at the range offset of every return. The waveform therefore adds no ray tracing, and every return costs the same as with the ideal sinc. The effective window is shared by all three imaging modalities.

\subsection{Radar Cross Section}
\label{sec:rcs}

The RCS of a target is the power it scatters back toward the sensor, as a function of look
direction, expressed as an equivalent area independent of range. It is the standard measure of how
visible a target is to a radar.

We compute it from one ray trace per look direction, without forming a sampled range signal. The ray grid of
Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim} is aimed at the origin from azimuth $a$ and elevation $e$, and its returns
are summed coherently,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{RCS}}(a,e) = \Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}\Bigg|^2
    ,
    \label{eq:rcs}
\end{equation}
where $E_k$ and $z_k$ are the return weights and two-way ranges of the $N$ returns traced at
$(a,e)$. Since $E_k$ is a fraction of the transmitted energy (Section \ref{sec:units}),
(\ref{eq:rcs}) is a relative RCS in arbitrary units.

Figure \ref{fig:rcs} shows (\ref{eq:rcs}) on a $2^\circ$ grid for a $1 \times 1$ flat plate and for
the car of Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.a without the ground, at the default $\lambda$. The plate
returns a lattice of sinc lobes around its normal. The car is strongest near the zenith and
broadside to its sides ($a = 0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$), and its ends return about $13$~dB less than
its sides at low elevation. Figure \ref{fig:rcs}c validates the phase of the ray-traced returns
against the physical-optics RCS of the plate: at both wavelengths the lobes and nulls fall where
physical optics puts them, and the envelope falls about $0.4$~dB faster at $80$--$85^\circ$
elevation, since the scattering lobe of the material is narrower than that of physical optics.

\subsection{Imaging Modalities}
\label{sec:modalities-overview}

The simulated signals are independent of how they are imaged, and the simulator forms images in
three modalities, summarized in Table \ref{tab:modalities} and detailed in Appendix
\ref{sec:modalities}. All three share the ray tracer, the material model of
(\ref{eq:returned-energy}), and the accumulation and interpolation of returns with any effective window $w(z)$, so the range
resolution of every modality is set by the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of the transmit waveform. They
differ in how the rays are launched, how the returns are weighted, and how the signals are formed
into an image.

\textbf{Spotlight-mode SAR} casts the parallel ray grid of Figure \ref{fig:sig-sim} from each
position of a circular or linear trajectory, aimed at the scene origin, and forms the image by
convolution backprojection \cite{spotlightmodesar}. When a wavelength is given, it demodulates
each pulse and integrates the complex-valued pulses coherently, so the image is formed from the phase of the returns as well as
their magnitude.

The sonars replace the planar wavefront with a point source. Each ping casts a fan of rays from the
sensor, every return is attenuated by spherical spreading, and the beam pattern of the sensor
weights each return on both transmit and receive. \textbf{Side-scan sonar} transmits its pings
along a straight track with a fixed beam, adds absorption in the water and a time-varying gain, and
stacks the range profiles along the track. \textbf{Forward-looking sonar} transmits from a single
position and steers its beam across the scene. Since the sensor does not move, the beam is applied
after ray tracing, and all $P$ pings share a single ray trace.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We test our signal simulation in the imaging modalities of Section \ref{sec:modalities-overview}. We first compare a constant material with per-triangle materials on a CVDomes car, then validate the SAR forward model on point scatterers and a sphere. The following experiments use spotlight-mode SAR as described in the Jakowatz book \cite{spotlightmodesar}, vary the bandwidth and sample rate to show where the signal model breaks down, and then measure the compute time on a GPU and a CPU. Further SAR images that vary the wavelength, the aperture, the noise, and the sensor trajectory are given in Appendix \ref{app:sar-examples}. The remaining experiments vary the azimuth rays of side-scan sonar and show how the ray density of a sonar fan changes across range, and finally compare the transmit waveforms in SAR and side-scan sonar. Sweeps of the sonar beam width are given in Appendix \ref{app:sonar-beam-width}. The sensor is placed on the bottom side of every image, so illumination always arrives from that direction. Unless specified otherwise, the SAR experiments use the default parameters shown in Table \ref{tab:params} of Appendix \ref{app:params}, and are on the car model from the ShapeNet \cite{shapenet} cars dataset shown in Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}.a.

\subsection{Bandwidth and Ray Density}
\label{sec:bw-ray-density}

The returned signal (\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) is a sum of delta functions, one per ray return,
and accumulating and interpolating the returns low-pass filters it with a pulse whose amplitude full width at half maximum is
$0.62/B_s$ for the default Gaussian waveform. A pulse that is wide against the range spacing of the
returns gives a smooth estimate of the continuous response. A narrow one resolves the returns, and
the gaps between them show up as nulls. In Figure \ref{fig:fsbw}, we lower the spatial bandwidth from
$B_s = 512$ to $64$ with $F_s = 2B_s$ at the default ray grids. Each column shows the sampled
signal of the middle SAR pulse, the SAR image, and the forward-looking sonar image. We then hold
$B_s = 64$ and scale the rays per side of both the SAR grid and the forward-looking sonar fan from
$0.125$ to $4$ times their defaults. Figure \ref{fig:raydensity} shows the signal of the middle SAR
pulse from $0.125$ to $1$ times the default rays, where it is already smooth. Both modalities image the same car from
the same pose, \texttt{pose\_num} 000000 at an elevation of $40.6^\circ$, the SAR sensor at the
pose's distance of $1.3\ell$ and the sonar at $7.5\ell$. All other settings are the defaults of
Tables \ref{tab:params} and \ref{tab:fls-params}, except that the SAR receiver noise is turned off
so that its floor does not hide the artifacts.

Figure \ref{fig:scatter-closeup} shows where the artifacts come from. The SAR ray grid is
orthographic, so on the flat seafloor the $R_w = 208$ rays in one row of the grid travel the same
distance and return at the same range, to within $1.1\times10^{-6}\ell$. The seafloor part of the
energy-range scatter is therefore a set of clusters of $208$ coincident returns, one per row. Rows
are $\Delta = W_g/R_h = 0.0058\ell$ apart on the grid, and $\Delta\cot\theta$ apart in range for a
pulse at elevation $\theta$, which is $0.0068\ell$ at the $40.6^\circ$ elevation of the middle pulse.
No bandwidth separates the returns within a cluster, since they share a range. The pulse does,
however, resolve the gaps between clusters once it is narrower than their spacing (Figure
\ref{fig:scatter-closeup}b). Over the seafloor window of Figure \ref{fig:scatter-closeup}b, the
ratio of the largest to the smallest signal sample is $0.0$, $5.6$, and $40$~dB at
$B_s = 64$, $128$, and $256$, whose pulses are $0.0097$, $0.0048$, and $0.0024\ell$ wide. At $B_s = 512$, the signal falls to zero between clusters, and its seafloor becomes a comb with
one peak per row of the ray grid.

The images follow their signals. At $B_s = 64$, the SAR image shows the outline of the car
over a dark seafloor. At $B_s = 128$ the outline breaks up, and at $256$ and $512$ the car is lost in
grain. The forward-looking sonar fails the same way along its
elevation fan. Its seafloor returns spread apart in range as the fan meets the seafloor at ever
shallower angles, from $0.0050\ell$ at the near edge of the image to $0.0078\ell$ at the far edge,
so from $B_s = 128$ on, where the pulse is $0.0048\ell$ wide, its seafloor breaks into concentric
rings centered on boresight. The side-scan sonar shows the same effect as horizontal stripes: every
ping sees the flat seafloor through the same fan, so its returns fall at the same ranges in every
ping, and a row that falls between them is dim along the whole track.

instead of widening the pulse. Doubling the rays per side halves $\Delta$, and with it the cluster

pulse falls to zero between clusters at $0.125\times$ rays, as it does at $B_s = 512$, and its

\ref{fig:scatter-closeup}c). On the seafloor on the far side of the car, the standard deviation of the forward-looking sonar image is
$9.6$ and $1.6$~dB at $0.25\times$ and $0.5\times$ rays, against $8.7$ and $1.5$~dB at
$B_s = 256$ and $128$ with the default fan. From $1\times$ rays on, both
signals are smooth, and the sonar's rings are gone.

The SAR image needs more rays than its middle pulse suggests. That pulse is smooth from $1\times$
on, but the grain the gaps leave in the image raises its seafloor: on the far seafloor beside the
car, the mean image level is $7.6$, $4.3$, $1.4$, $0.9$, and $0.9\%$ of the image peak from
$0.25\times$ to $4\times$ rays, so it settles only at $2\times$. The aperture is a linear track, so
its end pulses are $2.72\ell$ from the scene center against $1.30\ell$ for the middle pulse, and look
down at $18.1^\circ$ instead of $40.6^\circ$. Their $\cot\theta$ is $3.05$ instead of $1.17$, so
their clusters are $2.6$ times farther apart, $0.018\ell$ at the default rays. That spacing falls to
$0.0088\ell$ at $2\times$ and $0.0044\ell$ at $4\times$, below the $0.0097\ell$ pulse. A ray grid is therefore fine enough when the cluster spacing of
its most grazing look stays below the pulse width, $\Delta\cot\theta_{\min} < 0.62/B_s$, or
$R_h > 1.6\,B_s W_g \cot\theta_{\min}$, which is $375$ here, between the $1\times$ and $2\times$ grids.

Both remedies have a cost. Lowering $B_s$ coarsens the range resolution $1/B_s$. Scaling the rays
per side by $k$ scales the ray tracing time by $k^2$. On one NVIDIA L40S, tracing two bounces of
the $64$ SAR pulses of this car takes $117$, $468$, and $1909$~s at $208$, $416$, and $832$ rays per
side.

\subsection{Side-Scan Sonar Azimuth Rays}
\label{sec:ss-az-rays}

The remaining experiments test the two sonar modalities of Section \ref{sec:modalities-overview}
(Appendices \ref{sec:forward-looking} and \ref{sec:side-scan}) on the car of Figure
\ref{fig:main-fig}.a, with the same ray tracer, material model, and accumulation and interpolation of returns as the SAR
experiments. The car scatters almost entirely into the directional lobe and the seafloor mostly
diffusely, so the surfaces of the car that face the sensor return brightly against the seafloor,
and the car casts a dark acoustic shadow.

A side-scan sonar resolves the scene along the track with its beam, but the simulator can only
apply the beam to a finite fan of rays. We place the $R_w$ azimuth rays of each ping at uniform
angles $\phi_j$ over $\Theta_w = 3\beta$ (\ref{eq:ray-fan}), which covers the two-way beam out to
where it falls to $0.002$, and use an odd $R_w$ so that one ray lies on boresight. Uniform angles
give every ray an equal share of the fan, so the beam pattern (\ref{eq:beam-pattern}) alone sets
the weight of each ray. The fan is fixed to the sensor, so every ping casts its rays at the same
angles, and ray $j$ reaches a scatterer at slant range $\rho$ a distance $\rho\tan\phi_j$ along the
track from the ping that cast it. Over the pass, each ray therefore forms the image that a single
boresight ray would, shifted along the track by $\rho\tan\phi_j$ and weighted by the two-way beam
at $\phi_j$. The image is the sum of these $R_w$ copies, a sampling of the smear of a continuous
beam at a spacing of $\rho\Delta\phi$, with $\Delta\phi = 3\beta/(R_w-1)$. The copies blend into a
smear only when that spacing is below one along-track pixel, $S_\ell/W$, which at the far edge of
the image requires
\begin{equation}
    R_w > 1 + \frac{3\beta\,\rho_{far}\,W}{S_\ell}
    .
    \label{eq:ss-az-rays}
\end{equation}
Randomizing the angles from ping to ping would trade the copies for along-track noise, so we keep
them fixed and choose $R_w$ by (\ref{eq:ss-az-rays}). When the beam covers about a pixel, a single
boresight ray suffices, which is why the defaults of Table \ref{tab:sss-params} use $R_w = 1$ with
$\beta = 0.1^\circ$, where $\rho\beta$ is $1.4$ pixels.

In Figure \ref{fig:ss-az-rays}, we hold a wide beam of $\beta = 4^\circ$ and vary $R_w$ from $9$
to $501$, with the other parameters at the defaults of Table \ref{tab:sss-params}. The fan then
spans $12^\circ$, and (\ref{eq:ss-az-rays}) asks for $R_w > 182$. At the range of the car, the
copies are $21$, $7.0$, $2.6$, $0.9$, and $0.3$ pixels apart. Each panel is in dB relative to its own
peak, with a floor of $-60$~dB.

At $9$ rays, the beam does not smear the car but repeats it: copies of the car sit side by side
along the track, $1.5^\circ$ of the fan apart and dimmer the farther they are from boresight, and the
along-track autocorrelation of its rows peaks at a lag of $19$ pixels, against the $21$ predicted.
At $25$ rays the copies overlap into a comb of faint repeats, and at $65$ they persist as fine
vertical stripes across the car and its shadow. At $181$, at the bound of (\ref{eq:ss-az-rays}),
the image is a smooth smear along the track. Relative to the $501$-ray image, the $L_2$ error falls
from $25\%$ at $9$ rays to $7.5\%$, $2.9\%$, and $0.8\%$ at $181$. Too few
rays thus do not produce a blurrier image, but a wrong one whose repeats could be mistaken for
real targets, and the number of rays needed grows with both the beam width and the range.

\subsection{Sonar Ray Density Across Range}
\label{sec:sonar-range-density}

The SAR ray grid is orthographic, so its rays are spaced evenly over the scene. The rays of a sonar
fan instead leave a single point at uniform angular steps (\ref{eq:ray-fan}), and on a flat seafloor
uniform steps in angle are not uniform steps in range. A ray that meets the seafloor at a grazing
angle $\gamma$, from a sensor at height $h = \lVert\vec{v}\rVert\sin e$, returns from a one-way
range $\rho = h/\sin\gamma$. Neighboring elevation rays differ in $\gamma$ by
$\Delta\psi = \Theta_h/(R_h-1)$, so their returns are spaced in range by
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\rho = \bigg|\frac{d\rho}{d\gamma}\bigg|\,\Delta\psi = \rho\cot\gamma\,\Delta\psi
    = \frac{\rho\sqrt{\rho^2-h^2}}{h}\,\Delta\psi
    .
    \label{eq:sonar-ray-spacing}
\end{equation}
The spacing vanishes at nadir, where $\rho = h$, and grows as $\rho^2/h$ at long range, so each
azimuth ray of the fan puts $1/(F_s\Delta\rho)$ returns into a signal sample: many at near range,
and fewer than one at far range. This has two consequences. First, a pixel sums the energies of the
returns in its range cell, so the near seafloor is bright because more rays land there. The fan
samples solid angle uniformly, so the number of rays in a range cell follows the solid angle the cell
subtends from the sensor, which is the share of the transmitted energy it intercepts. Second, the far
seafloor is undersampled. Once $\Delta\rho$ exceeds the pulse width, the gaps between returns of
Section \ref{sec:bw-ray-density} appear at far range, while near range stays smooth.

To show both, we render one forward-looking sonar image whose rows run from the sensor out to twice
the range of the car, $\rho_{near} = 0.016$ to $\rho_{far} = 14.98$. The $M = 959$ rows are
$1/F_s$ apart, one per signal sample, so the rows do not beat against the samples. The fan spans
$\Theta_h = 100^\circ$ in elevation with $R_h = 1040$ rays, so $\Delta\psi = 0.096^\circ$. At the
elevation $e = 40.6^\circ$ of \texttt{pose\_num} 000000, the fan needs
$2(90^\circ - e) = 98.9^\circ$ to reach nadir, and the sensor height is $h = 4.88$. The image spans
$\Theta_p = 2$~rad $= 114.6^\circ$ in azimuth, so that its arc at the range of the car,
$\Theta_p\lVert\vec{v}\rVert = 15$, matches its range axis and the car keeps its proportions. To
keep the beam and the azimuth rays at the default spacing relative to the pings, the $P = 512$ pings
steer a beam of $\beta = 0.31^\circ$, and the fan has $R_w = 1299$ rays over
$\Theta_w = \Theta_p + 3\beta = 115.5^\circ$. All other parameters are the defaults of Table
\ref{tab:fls-params}: a range of $\lVert\vec{v}\rVert = 7.5$ and a Gaussian pulse with $B_s = 32$
and $F_s = 64$. Figure \ref{fig:fls-range-density} shows the image in dB relative to its peak, with
a floor of $-80$~dB.

Nothing returns from before $\rho = h$, the water column between the sensor and the seafloor. The
image peaks just past it, at $\rho = 4.89$. At nadir $\Delta\rho$ falls to second order,
$h\Delta\psi^2/2 = 7\times10^{-6}$, so over $45$ elevation rays from the last $4.6^\circ$ of the fan
land within one sample of nadir. Beyond nadir, the seafloor darkens steadily. Between $5^\circ$ and
$15^\circ$ off boresight, beside the car, its median level is $-23.8$~dB at $\rho = 5.5$,
$-40.4$~dB at the range of the car, $\rho = 7.5$, and $-65.7$~dB at $\rho = 14$. The brightest
return of the car, at $-6.7$~dB, is weaker than the nadir line. Near boresight, the first-bounce
returns of the fan per sample and per azimuth ray number $3.4$ at $\rho = 5.5$, $1.1$ at $7.5$,
$0.52$ at $10$, and $0.21$ at $15$, within $3\%$ of $1/(F_s\Delta\rho)$. From $\rho = 6$ to $12$
the seafloor falls by $28.7$~dB. The image is shown as $20\log_{10}$ of a pixel, and a pixel is
linear in the energies it sums. Over that span the returns per sample fall $6.4$-fold
($16.2$~dB), the spreading loss of (\ref{eq:sonar-energy}) falls $4$-fold ($12.0$~dB), and the
energy of a return before spreading falls by $15\%$ ($1.2$~dB), $29.4$~dB in total. More than half
of the fall-off is therefore the density of the rays rather than the spreading loss.

The bright nadir line only spans the middle of the image. Off boresight, the plane of a column of
the fan does not contain the vertical, so its closest point on the seafloor lies at a more negative
$\psi$ than on boresight. Beyond $|\phi_i| \approx 12^\circ$, that point lies past the
$\psi = -50^\circ$ edge of the fan. The near edge of the image is then the edge of the fan, which
meets the seafloor farther out the farther it is off boresight. It curves from $\rho = 4.89$ on
boresight to $5.42$ at $40^\circ$ and $6.05$ at the edges of the image. Without the pile-up of rays
at nadir, it dims, to $-12.7$~dB at $40^\circ$ and $-22.0$~dB at the edges.

The same thinning undersamples the far seafloor. At the range of the car,
$\Delta\rho = 0.0147$ is $0.76$ of the $0.62/B_s = 0.019$ width of the pulse, so the returns still
overlap and the seafloor is smooth. $\Delta\rho$ reaches the pulse width at $\rho = 8.3$ and twice
it at $\rho = 11.2$, and beyond $\rho \approx 8.5$ the seafloor near boresight breaks into
concentric arcs.
These are the rings of Figure \ref{fig:fsbw} at high $B_s$, now driven by range within a single
image instead of by bandwidth. Just beside the car, $2.5^\circ$ to $3.5^\circ$ off boresight, the
standard deviation of the seafloor about its local mean, in dB over windows $0.5$ long, is at most
$0.02$~dB out to the car at $\rho = 7.5$, and $0.15$~dB at $\rho = 8$, where $\Delta\rho$ is $0.90$
pulse widths. It is $0.47$~dB at $\rho = 8.5$, $1.0$ at $9$, $2.7$ at $10$, $8.4$ at $12$, and
$16$~dB at $14$, where $\Delta\rho$ is $3.3$ pulse widths.

The arcs fade off boresight because of the beam. A row of the fan at a fixed $\psi$ meets the
seafloor farther out the farther it is from boresight, so its range changes across the beam of one
ping. On boresight the row is flat, and the gaps between rows reach the image. Off boresight, the
beam spans rows over a range that grows with $|\phi_i|$, and once that range covers one spacing
$\Delta\rho$ the beam fills the gaps. At $\rho = 14$, the range spanned by one beam width is
$0.18\Delta\rho$ at $5^\circ$, $0.75\Delta\rho$ at $20^\circ$, and $1.2\Delta\rho$ at $30^\circ$,
and the deviation falls from $17$~dB within $1^\circ$ of boresight to $13$~dB at $5^\circ$,
$1.1$~dB at $20^\circ$, and $0.38$~dB at $30^\circ$.

The condition of Section \ref{sec:bw-ray-density} carries over with the sonar's spacing: the image
is smooth when $\Delta\rho$ at its far edge stays below the pulse width, or
$R_h > \Theta_h\,\rho_{far}\cot\gamma_{\min}\,B_s/0.62$, with $\Theta_h$ in radians. That is $3900$
elevation rays for this window, $3.8$ times the rays used here. Since $\Delta\rho$ grows as
$\rho^2$, the far edge of the window sets the rays needed, and most of the rays added to smooth it
land at near range, where there are already $16$ times as many returns per sample as at
$\rho = 15$. The elevation field of view enters the same way. A wide fan is needed to image near
range, but at a fixed $R_h$ widening $\Theta_h$ raises $\Delta\psi$, and with it $\Delta\rho$, at
every range. The default fan of Table \ref{tab:fls-params} spans $23^\circ$ with $550$ rays over a
window from $\rho = 6.9$ to $8.1$, so its spacing at the far edge is $0.0078$, $0.40$ pulse widths.

\subsection{Transmit Waveform}
\label{sec:waveform-experiment}

In Figure \ref{fig:waveform}, we vary the transmit waveform of spotlight-mode SAR (top row) and
side-scan sonar (bottom row), which replaces the ideal sinc of (\ref{eq:accum-interp}) with the effective
window $w(z)$ of the corresponding pulse-compression matched filter as described in Section
\ref{sec:waveforms} and illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}. Within each row the ray
trace is identical across the panels, and only the effective window changes. We compare an ideal
sinc, a Gaussian pulse, an LFM chirp, and a Barker-13 phase code, all designed for the same spatial
bandwidth $B_s = 30$, and then the LFM chirp at $B_s = 60$, with $F_s = 2B_s$ throughout. The SAR
images use the defaults of Table \ref{tab:params} and are shown linearly relative to their own
peak. The side-scan images use the defaults of Table \ref{tab:sss-params} and, as in Figure
\ref{fig:ss-az-rays}, are shown in dB relative to their own peak with a floor of $-60$~dB.

In SAR, the sinc, LFM chirp, and Barker-13 reconstructions share a comparable range resolution, and
their range sidelobes appear only as faint replicas alongside the strong scatterers. The background
of the image is nearly unchanged by the waveform: its $10$th percentile lies between $-43.2$ and
$-44.6$~dB relative to the peak across the four waveforms at $B_s = 30$. The Gaussian pulse
produces no range sidelobes, but it does a poor job of suppressing out-of-bandwidth frequencies,
leaving much of its energy outside the allotted band $\pm B_s/2$. Its gradual roll-off also tapers
the in-band response, which costs coherent gain and yields the dimmer peak of its panel. Doubling
the bandwidth of the chirp halves its mainlobe and sharpens the outline of the car.

In side-scan sonar, the range sidelobes of the pulse-compression waveforms are far more intense.
With the Gaussian pulse, the car casts a clean acoustic shadow at far range whose median amplitude
lies $75$~dB below the seafloor beside it. Every other waveform fills that shadow, to $12.2$~dB
below the seafloor for the sinc, $9.1$~dB for the LFM chirp, and $6.9$~dB for Barker-13, and
doubling the bandwidth of the chirp still leaves it at $12.1$~dB. The sidelobes also ring through
the car as bands of constant range, and trail streaks in range from its brightest glints across
the full height of the image. Barker-13 further lifts the seafloor itself, from $31$~dB to $25$~dB
below the peak.

Three properties of the side-scan sonar make its sidelobes stronger than those of SAR. First, each
column of a side-scan image is the matched-filter output of a single ping, so the range sidelobes
of every return enter the image directly. In SAR, each pixel coherently sums $64$ pulses spread over
$135^\circ$ of aspect. The sidelobes of a scatterer fall on a different iso-range arc in each
pulse, so they spread thinly over the image, while the mainlobes add up at the scatterer. Second,
the sidelobes are long compared with the $1.2$ range swath of the image. The sidelobes of the sinc
decay as $1/(\pi B_s z)$ and stay above $-60$~dB out to $z \approx 10$, the LFM chirp with a
time-bandwidth product of $100$ spreads over $100/B_s \approx 3.3$, and the flat $-22.3$~dB
sidelobes of Barker-13 span $\pm 13/B_s \approx \pm 0.43$. Against the dB display, the sidelobes of
the bright car therefore exceed the seafloor and the shadow over most of the image. Third, the
side-scan sonar is given no carrier wavelength, so its returns add without their carrier phase and
their sidelobes cannot cancel. The aperiodic autocorrelation sidelobes of Barker-13 are all
non-negative, so across the densely sampled seafloor they accumulate rather than alternate in sign
as those of the sinc do, which raises the seafloor relative to the peak. For these reasons the
side-scan and forward-looking sonar defaults use the Gaussian pulse.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The simulator trades physical fidelity for speed and generality. Five simplifications bound what
its images can be used for.

\textbf{Scalar returns.} Each return keeps only its energy, path length, and round-trip phase.
Unlike SBR (Section \ref{sec:sbr}), no polarization is carried along the ray, so the simulator
produces a single channel and cannot form HH, VV, or HV images or polarimetric decompositions. The
double-bounce returns between the car and the ground, which change the polarization of a real
wave, differ from single-bounce returns only in their delay. Carrying a polarization vector with
each ray and reflecting it with Fresnel coefficients at each bounce would add polarization without
changing the ray trace.

\textbf{Geometrical optics.} Rays reflect into their next path by geometrical optics and return
energy through the phenomenological lobe of Section \ref{sec:signal-simulation}. No surface
currents are integrated, so edge and tip diffraction, creeping waves, and resonant scattering from
features comparable in size to $\lambda$ are not modeled, and the model holds only for targets much
larger than the wavelength. The wavelength enters only through the phase of each return, so the
scattering of a surface does not depend on frequency. The flat plate of Section \ref{sec:rcs}
matches physical optics in its lobes and nulls because the coherent sum reproduces the phase
across the plate, but its $\sigma$ matches only up to the aperture gain $4\pi/\lambda^2$. Rays are
traced through a fixed number of bounces, two in our experiments, so multipath of higher order than
\texttt{num\_bounce}, such as returns that bounce many times inside a cavity, is missing, and each
added bounce adds another pass of the ray trace. Returns are summed as $E_k$ rather than $\sqrt{E_k}$, and no
antenna gain, transmit power, or noise figure is modeled (Section \ref{sec:units}), so image values
are not calibrated radar cross sections. For sonar, the water is homogeneous: there is no
sound-speed profile or refraction, no sea-surface multipath, and no volume reverberation, and the
target scatters as a rigid surface without the elastic resonances of real objects. Returns lose
energy only to spherical spreading and, in side-scan sonar, to a constant absorption $\kappa$.

\textbf{Ray density.} The returns are a discrete sample of a continuous surface, and the signal is
smooth only when the range spacing between them stays below the pulse width at the most grazing
look, $R_h > 1.6\,B_sW_g\cot\theta_{\min}$ for SAR (Section \ref{sec:bw-ray-density}) and its
analogue at the far edge of a sonar fan (Section \ref{sec:sonar-range-density}). Below this density
the images break into combs, rings, and stripes rather than blurring. Meeting it is costly: the
SAR rule asks for $375$ rays per side against the default $208$, imaging the forward-looking sonar
out to $\rho = 15$ needs $3900$ elevation rays, and the ray tracing time grows as the square of the
rays per side, from $117$ to $1909$~s between $1\times$ and $4\times$ the default SAR grid. The
orthographic grid places the seafloor returns of each row at a single range, so a jittered or
stratified grid would spread them apart and may lower the density required.

\textbf{Materials.} The fractions $(R,A,I,D,S)$ and the exponent $\alpha$ are phenomenological.
They are not derived from permittivity, conductivity, or surface roughness, and they do not vary
with the angle of incidence or the frequency, as Fresnel reflection would. ShapeNet meshes carry
no material labels, so our ShapeNet experiments use one material for the car and one for the
ground (Table \ref{tab:materials}). Per-triangle materials require a mapping from labels to
$(R,A,I,D,S)$ chosen by hand, as in the CVDomes conversion of Section
\ref{sec:per-triangle-materials}, where glass is given a single band-averaged reflection. No ray is
transmitted through a surface, so the interior of a vehicle is never seen through its windows.

\textbf{Validation.} The forward model is validated against point scatterers (Section \ref{sec:validation}) and the
physical-optics glint of a flat plate (Section \ref{sec:rcs}), but its images are not
compared against measured SAR or sonar data, or quantitatively against CVDomes. Its images should
therefore be read as reproducing the geometry of real sensors, including shadows and
multipath, rather than their calibrated intensities.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we presented an open-source, GPU-accelerated SAR and sonar simulator implemented
in PyTorch that supports arbitrary 3D \texttt{.obj} models, multi-bounce ray tracing, and
configurable sensor parameters such as wavelength, bandwidth, sample rate, transmit waveform, and
material properties. From a single ray-traced signal model it renders three imaging modalities:
spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan sonar, and forward-looking sonar. It addresses the
limitations of existing open tools: \emph{RaySAR} relies on outdated software such as POV-Ray and
MATLAB and does not model a transmit waveform, \emph{CVDomes} is a fixed dataset of ten vehicles
whose simulation code is not public, and
neither simulates sonar.

The proposed band-limited \emph{accumulation and interpolation} of ray returns is an efficient
low-pass filter that accounts for the sensor's bandwidth and sampling
rate. Rather than range-compressing an already sampled signal, it evaluates the band-limited
response directly at the sample points from the off-grid ray returns, so interpolation and summation
happen in one step. This keeps the signal band-limited at the sensor's bandwidth instead of
interpolating returns onto the sample grid, as long as the rays are dense enough to sample the
surface (Section \ref{sec:limitations}). The technique extends to arbitrary transmit waveforms
by replacing its ideal sinc with the effective window of the waveform's matched filter, so sinc,
Gaussian, LFM chirp, Barker-13, or user-defined waveforms are simulated from the same ray trace at
the same cost, with the range sidelobes of a real pulse-compression system. When a carrier wavelength is given,
every return keeps its round-trip phase, and SAR images are formed by coherent integration of the
complex-valued signals, which we validated against ideal point scatterers and the physical-optics
glint of a flat plate.

We conducted a series of experiments on ShapeNet car models in spotlight-mode SAR, side-scan
sonar, and forward-looking sonar, while varying key parameters such as bandwidth, sample rate, and beam width, with
further SAR examples varying the aperture, wavelength, and sensor trajectory in
Appendix \ref{app:sar-examples}. These experiments demonstrate how system
parameters influence image quality and signal fidelity. In particular, we observed in both SAR and
sonar that once the pulse is narrower than the range spacing between ray returns, the signal
resolves the individual returns and the images break into artifacts. This can be avoided either by
reducing the effective bandwidth, which widens the pulse, or by increasing the ray density, which
moves the returns closer together.
Future work will extend the simulator to arbitrary flight paths and synthetic aperture sonar, carry
polarization along each ray, randomize the ray grid to lower the ray density required, and compare
its images against measured data.

\section{Imaging Modalities}
\label{sec:modalities}

Any imaging algorithm may be applied to the simulated signals, and this section describes the three
used in this work: forward-looking sonar, side-scan sonar, and spotlight-mode SAR.
When a carrier wavelength $\lambda$ is specified, each return carries the round-trip phase of its
own path, and
the energy-range scatter of (\ref{eq:returned-scatters}) becomes complex,
\begin{equation}
    g(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}\delta(z-z_k)
    .
    \label{eq:coherent-scatters}
\end{equation}
The returns are accumulated and interpolated as before, so the digital signal of the $i^{th}$ pulse, sampled on the
one-way range axis, is
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}E_ke^{j 4 \pi \zeta_k / \lambda}w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:coherent-signal}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_k = z_k/2$ is the one-way range of the $k^{th}$ return.

\subsection{Forward-Looking Sonar}
\label{sec:forward-looking}

A forward-looking sonar image is formed from $P$ pings, the sonar term for pulses, all transmitted
from a single position with the beam steered to a different azimuth for each. The sensor sits at
$\vec{v}$, given by an azimuth $a$ and elevation $e$ as in Section \ref{sec:problem}, and its
boresight points at the scene origin. Range is measured from the delay of each return, and azimuth
is measured by steering the beam pattern of the sensor. Because every quantity in our simulator is
expressed in spatial units, the same model applies to sonar and radar alike.

Since the sensor is a point rather than a plane, the parallel $W_g \times H_g$ ray grid of the
signal simulation is replaced by a fan of $R_h \times R_w$ rays that all leave $\vec{v}$. Let
$\vec{u}_f = -\vec{v}/\lVert\vec{v}\rVert$ be the boresight direction, and let $\vec{u}_h$ and
$\vec{u}_v$ be the horizontal and vertical axes of the sensor. A ray launched at an azimuth
offset $\phi$ and an elevation offset $\psi$ from boresight has direction
\begin{equation}
    \vec{d}(\phi,\psi) = \cos(\psi)\cos(\phi)\,\vec{u}_f + \cos(\psi)\sin(\phi)\,\vec{u}_h + \sin(\psi)\,\vec{u}_v
    ,
    \label{eq:ray-fan}
\end{equation}
where $R_w$ values of $\phi$ are spaced uniformly over $[-\Theta_w/2,\Theta_w/2]$ and $R_h$ values
of $\psi$ over $[-\Theta_h/2,\Theta_h/2]$, and $\Theta_w \times \Theta_h$ is the angular field of
view of the fan. Parameterizing the fan by angle rather than by a flat image plane keeps the
angular sampling uniform, which matters because the beam pattern is applied in angle.

Ray tracing the fan yields the same energy-range pairs $(E_k,z_k)$ as before, with $z_k$ now the
true round-trip distance from $\vec{v}$. A point source loses energy over the surface of an
expanding sphere, which a planar wavefront does not, so each return is attenuated by the spreading
loss over its round-trip range. With the carrier
phase of (\ref{eq:coherent-scatters}), the energy of the $k^{th}$ return is
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{E}_k = \frac{E_k}{4 \pi z_k^2}e^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:sonar-energy}
\end{equation}
and the phasor is omitted when no wavelength is given.

Each return also carries two azimuths off boresight. Its transmit azimuth $\phi^{tx}_k$ is the
launch azimuth $\phi$ of the ray that produced it. Its receive azimuth $\phi^{rx}_k$ is the
azimuth of the point $\vec{p}_k$ that scattered it back to the sensor,
\begin{equation}
    \phi^{rx}_k = \operatorname{atan2}\big(\vec{u}_h\cdot(\vec{p}_k-\vec{v}),\,\vec{u}_f\cdot(\vec{p}_k-\vec{v})\big)
    .
    \label{eq:rx-azimuth}
\end{equation}
The two are equal for a first-bounce return. For a return from a later bounce, $\phi^{tx}_k$ is the
direction of the ray's first path, while $\phi^{rx}_k$ is the direction of the point of contact
that scattered it back, which is where a real receiver hears the energy arrive from.

The sensor cannot resolve the bearing of a single return, so each return is weighted by the beam
pattern on both transmit and receive. We model the one-way beam as a Gaussian with full width at
half maximum $\sqrt{2}\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    b(\phi) = \exp\bigg(-\frac{\phi^2}{2\sigma_\beta^2}\bigg)
    , \quad
    \sigma_\beta = \frac{\sqrt{2}\beta}{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}}
    ,
    \label{eq:beam-pattern}
\end{equation}
and weight the $k^{th}$ return by $b(\phi^{tx}_k)\,b(\phi^{rx}_k)$. For a first-bounce return this
product is a Gaussian with full width at half maximum $\beta$, the two-way beam width. The pattern
peaks at $1$ rather than integrating to $1$, so a return on boresight keeps its full energy, and
widening the beam spreads a return over more pings without dividing its energy among them.

Ping $i$ steers both beams to an azimuth $\phi_i$, where the $P$ values of $\phi_i$ are spaced
uniformly over $[-\Theta_p/2,\Theta_p/2]$. Steering shifts both patterns by $\phi_i$, and the
digital signal of the $i^{th}$ ping is the accumulation and interpolation of the weighted returns,
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{E}_k\,b\big(\phi^{tx}_k-\phi_i\big)\,b\big(\phi^{rx}_k-\phi_i\big)\,w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:fls-signal}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_k = z_k/2$ is the one-way range of the $k^{th}$ return. The beam is applied after ray
tracing rather than during it, and the sensor does not move between pings, so every ping shares
the same returns and differs only in their weights. The fan is therefore ray traced once, and the
$P$ signals each need their own accumulation and interpolation but share a single ray trace.

The image has $M$ rows at one-way ranges $\rho_m$, spaced uniformly from $\rho_{near}$ in the
bottom row to $\rho_{far}$ in the top row, and one column per ping, with $\phi_i$ increasing from
left to right. The pixel at range $\rho_m$ and azimuth $\phi_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    I(\rho_m,\phi_i) = \big|s_i(\rho_m)\big|
    ,
    \label{eq:fls-image}
\end{equation}
where $|s_i|$ is evaluated between its samples by linear interpolation, and every signal is
sampled over a window that covers $[\rho_{near},\rho_{far}]$. The rows span a range swath
$S_\rho$ that brackets the scene origin along the line of sight, $\rho_{near} =
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert - S_\rho/2$ and $\rho_{far} = \lVert\vec{v}\rVert + S_\rho/2$, so the window
follows the sensor when it is moved away from the scene.

In range, the resolution of (\ref{eq:fls-signal}) is set by the spatial bandwidth $B_s$ of $w(z)$,
as in SAR, rather than by the spacing of the rows. In azimuth, it is set by the beam width $\beta$
rather than by a synthetic aperture, and the pings sample it every $\Theta_p/(P-1)$. The azimuth
span of the image $\Theta_p$ is separate from the field of view $\Theta_w$ of the fan, and should
be narrower than it by about $3\beta$. The two-way beam falls to about $0.002$ at $1.5\beta$ off
its axis, so this margin puts rays under the whole beam of every ping, including the pings at the
edges of the image. A fan narrower than that leaves the outer pings with part of their beam unlit,
and the edges of the image darken. When $\lambda$ is given, the returns sum coherently within each
ping, so interference between nearby rays appears as speckle.

\subsection{Side-Scan Sonar}
\label{sec:side-scan}

A side-scan sonar image is formed from $P$ pings transmitted along a straight track, with a beam
that is never steered. The platform flies through a mean position $\bar{\vec{v}}$, given by $a$
and $e$ as in Section \ref{sec:problem}, and every ping looks in the same direction, the boresight
$\vec{u}_f = -\bar{\vec{v}}/\lVert\bar{\vec{v}}\rVert$ at the center of the track. The track runs
along the horizontal axis of the sensor, $\vec{u}_h = (\vec{u}_f \times \vec{u}_z)/\lVert\vec{u}_f
\times \vec{u}_z\rVert$, where $\vec{u}_z$ is the unit vector along the $z$ axis, so the scene
stays off to one side of the platform the whole way. Ping $i$ is transmitted from
\begin{equation}
    \vec{v}_i = \bar{\vec{v}} + \ell_i\vec{u}_h
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-track}
\end{equation}
where the $P$ offsets $\ell_i$ are spaced uniformly over $[-L/2,L/2]$ for a track of length $L$.
Where a forward-looking sonar sweeps its beam across the target from one position, a side-scan
sonar holds its beam fixed, and the target drifts across the beam as the platform passes.

Each ping casts its own fan (\ref{eq:ray-fan}) from $\vec{v}_i$, and every fan shares the axes
$\vec{u}_f$, $\vec{u}_h$ and $\vec{u}_v = \vec{u}_h \times \vec{u}_f$. The fan spans $\Theta_w =
3\beta$ in azimuth, since the two-way beam has fallen to about $0.002$ at its edges, and
$\Theta_h$ in elevation, which sets the swath of seafloor that is imaged. The returns take the
energy of (\ref{eq:sonar-energy}) with an added absorption loss over their round trip through the
water,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{E}_k = \frac{E_k e^{-\kappa z_k}}{4 \pi z_k^2}e^{j 2 \pi z_k / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-energy}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the absorption coefficient in nepers per $\ell$. The spreading loss
$4 \pi z_k^2$ can be disabled by setting \texttt{spherical\_spread} to False in the side-scan
configuration, as in Table \ref{tab:sss-params}. Their azimuths
$\phi^{tx}_k$ and $\phi^{rx}_k$ are measured off the fixed boresight as in (\ref{eq:rx-azimuth}),
with $\vec{v}_i$ in place of $\vec{v}$, so the azimuth of a return locates it along the track
rather than always lying near $0$.

The beam pattern (\ref{eq:beam-pattern}) is applied as in forward-looking sonar, but without
steering, and the digital signal of the $i^{th}$ ping is
\begin{equation}
    s_i(z_o) = z_o^{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{E}_k\,b\big(\phi^{tx}_k\big)\,b\big(\phi^{rx}_k\big)\,w\big(z_o-\zeta_k\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:ss-signal}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the returns of that ping. The factor $z_o^{\eta}$ is a time-varying gain, a
receiver ramp against the fall of the seafloor return with range, and $\eta = 0$ disables it.
Every ping is sampled over the same window, which covers the range swath of the image, so the
samples of all pings line up in range.

Stacked as columns, the magnitudes $|s_i(z_o)|$ sample the scene over along-track offset $\ell_i$
and slant range $z_o$. The image interpolates them bilinearly onto an $H \times W$ pixel grid that
spans a cross-range swath $S_\ell$ along the track and a range swath $S_\rho$ in slant range,
centered on $\ell = 0$ and on the target range $\lVert\bar{\vec{v}}\rVert$, with near range in the
bottom row. Pixels outside the sampled track are zero, so the track length $L$ should be at least
$S_\ell$. The rows are in slant range, since no projection onto the ground plane is applied.

Along the track, the resolution is set by the beam: at slant range $\rho$ the beam covers about
$\rho\beta$ of the track, with $\beta$ in radians, so a narrower beam resolves finer detail. In
range, it is set by $B_s$ as before. The seafloor is seen at a grazing angle, so an object standing
off it occludes the ground down range and casts an acoustic shadow, and the top of the object
returns before the seafloor beneath it because it is closer to the sensor.

\subsection{Spotlight-Mode SAR}
\label{sec:spotlight-sar}

A spotlight-mode SAR image is formed by convolution backprojection \cite{spotlightmodesar}. The radar
transmits $P$ pulses from positions $\vec{v}_i$, at azimuth $a_i$ and elevation $e_i$, and every
pulse is aimed at the scene origin. The wavefront of the signal simulation is planar, so the
iso-range surfaces of pulse $i$ are planes normal to its look direction $\vec{u}_i =
-\vec{v}_i/\lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert$, and a point $\vec{x}$ on the ground plane returns at the
one-way range
\begin{equation}
    z_o = \lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert + \vec{u}_i\cdot\vec{x}
    .
    \label{eq:plane-wave-range}
\end{equation}
Only the horizontal part of $\vec{u}_i$ contributes to (\ref{eq:plane-wave-range}), and its length
is $\cos e_i$. Letting $\vec{n}_i$ be its unit vector, the ground direction of pulse $i$, the
sample at range $z_o$ comes from the ground line $\vec{n}_i\cdot\vec{x} = r$, where
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{z_o-\lVert\vec{v}_i\rVert}{\cos e_i}
    .
    \label{eq:ground-projection}
\end{equation}
Each pulse is therefore a projection of the scene reflectivity onto the line through the origin
along $\vec{n}_i$, sampled at the ground-plane rate $F_s\cos e_i$, and the image is recovered from
the $P$ projections as in computed tomography. The projection is undefined at $e_i = 90^\circ$,
where the pulse looks straight down and has no ground direction.

When a wavelength is given, each pulse is first demodulated,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}_i(z) = s_i(z)e^{-j 4 \pi z / \lambda}
    ,
    \label{eq:demod}
\end{equation}
which removes the carrier of (\ref{eq:coherent-signal}) and integrates the pulses coherently.
Without a wavelength the signals are real and this step is skipped. Expressed in the ground
coordinate $r$ of (\ref{eq:ground-projection}), each pulse is filtered by weighting its spectrum
$\tilde{S}_i(Z)$ by the ramp $|Z|$,
\begin{equation}
    \check{s}_i(r) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|Z|\tilde{S}_i(Z)e^{j 2 \pi r Z}dZ
    ,
    \label{eq:cbp-ramp}
\end{equation}
and the filtered pulses are backprojected along their ground lines,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\gamma}(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\check{s}_i\big(\vec{n}_i\cdot\vec{x}\big)
    ,
    \label{eq:cbp}
\end{equation}
where $\check{s}_i$ is evaluated between its samples by linear interpolation, or by sinc
interpolation at the rate $F_s\cos e_i$. The ramp is the Jacobian of the polar-to-Cartesian change
of variables in the image's spatial-frequency plane, and the factor $1/4\pi^2$ comes from the
inverse two-dimensional Fourier transform in polar coordinates \cite{spotlightmodesar}. The image
samples $\vec{x}$ on an $H \times W$ grid of the ground plane centered on the origin, rotated so
that the radar illuminates it from the bottom edge, and the displayed image is
$|\hat{\gamma}(\vec{x})|$.

In forward-looking sonar, the beam rather than the $0.072^\circ$ ping spacing sets the azimuth
resolution at every width. The edge of the shadow widens with $\beta$, the shadow fills in once the
beam is wider than its $3^\circ$ span, and at $10^\circ$ the returns of the car spread into arcs of
constant range. The fan is fixed, so a wider beam sums the returns of more rays into each ping, and
the peak grows about in proportion to $\beta$ up to $1^\circ$.

In side-scan sonar, a beam covers $\rho\beta$ of the track, $1.4$ pixels at $0.1^\circ$ and more than
the whole image at $10^\circ$. As it widens, the car smears along the track into streaks at constant
range, and its shadow fills in with the seafloor heard past the ends of the car. The fan spans
$3\beta$ with $R_w = 250$ azimuth rays, so the same number of rays falls under the beam at every
width, and the peak falls rather than rises as the brightest returns are averaged with their
surroundings. At $10^\circ$, $250$ rays fall short of the $450$ of (\ref{eq:ss-az-rays}), and faint
stripes of Section \ref{sec:ss-az-rays} remain. In both sonars, range resolution is set by $B_s$ and
does not change with $\beta$.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}
The simulator is the \texttt{sarsonar} Python package in the code repository, installed from a clone with \texttt{pip install -e ".[experiments]"}.
The experimental figures and tables are produced by the scripts in its \texttt{experiments/} folder, each run from that folder with no arguments.
The scripts read their defaults from \texttt{experiments/config.json} (Appendix \ref{app:params}), whose \texttt{srn\_cars\_dir}, \texttt{shapenet\_cars\_dir} and \texttt{cvdomes\_dir} point to the SRN Cars dataset \cite{srn}, the ShapeNet \cite{shapenet} car meshes and the CVDomes \cite{cvdomes} models, and write to \texttt{experiments/figures/}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:modality-comparison}: \texttt{modality\_comparison.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:main-fig}: \texttt{sar\_overview\_figure.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:effective-windows}: \texttt{window\_analysis.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:materials}: \texttt{convert\_cvdomes.py}, then \texttt{render\_cvdomes.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:validation} and the validation numbers: \texttt{sar\_validation.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:rcs}: \texttt{calculate\_rcs.py} (a, b) and \texttt{sar\_validation.py} (c)
  \item Figures \ref{fig:sphere-size} and \ref{fig:wavelength}--\ref{fig:trajectory-noise}: \texttt{sar\_paper\_figures.py}
  \item Figures \ref{fig:fsbw}--\ref{fig:scatter-closeup}: \texttt{fsbw\_and\_raydensity\_exp.py}
  \item Table \ref{tab:compute-time}: \texttt{compute\_time\_experiment.py}
  \item Figures \ref{fig:ss-az-rays} and \ref{fig:ss-beam-width}: \texttt{sss\_paper\_figures.py}
  \item Figures \ref{fig:fls-range-density} and \ref{fig:fls-beam-width}: \texttt{fls\_paper\_figures.py}
  \item Figure \ref{fig:waveform}: \texttt{waveform\_figure.py}
\end{itemize}
The sweep scripts \texttt{sar\_paper\_figures.py}, \texttt{sss\_paper\_figures.py} and \texttt{fls\_paper\_figures.py} take sweep names to render only those, and with \texttt{fsbw\_and\_raydensity\_exp.py} and \texttt{waveform\_figure.py} take \texttt{-replot} to redraw a figure from the outputs saved on an earlier run without rendering again.
